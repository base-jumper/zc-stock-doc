# Autonomous Vehicles

## Thesis

Widespread autonomous vehicle rollout will significantly reduce the cost of hailing a ride by removing the human driver from the cost stack and improving fleet utilisation. As ride-hailing costs fall, demand should increase. In dense urban and suburban areas, robotaxi services may eventually become cheaper and more convenient than private vehicle ownership for many daily use cases.

The strongest version of the thesis is not simply “self-driving cars exist”. It is that autonomous fleets become cheap, reliable, and available enough to change consumer behaviour: fewer households owning second cars, some households abandoning car ownership entirely, and more transport demand flowing through ride-hailing/logistics platforms.

## Initial Probability

- By 2030: ~50% that robotaxis are a meaningful commercial category in multiple major cities.
- By 2035: ~70% that autonomous ride-hailing is materially changing transport behaviour in at least some markets.

This is the most execution-dependent FIT. The technology is real, but rollout may be city-by-city rather than universal. Dense urban markets likely come first. Suburbs take longer. Rural areas may never fully fit the model.

## Why This Could Happen

- Human drivers are a major cost component in ride-hailing.
- Autonomous fleets can potentially run longer hours and be positioned dynamically based on demand.
- Younger consumers may be less attached to car ownership if mobility is cheap and reliable.
- Cities may prefer fewer parked private vehicles and more efficient transport utilisation.
- Delivery/logistics applications can improve the economics even if passenger ride-hailing ramps slowly.

## Key Conditions / Enablers

- Safety performance clearly better than human drivers.
- Regulatory approval in major cities.
- Insurance costs per autonomous mile falling.
- Vehicle capex and maintenance low enough to support attractive unit economics.
- High utilisation: autonomous fleets need to stay busy.
- Consumer trust and repeat usage.
- Charging/refuelling, cleaning, maintenance, and depot infrastructure.
- Mapping/simulation/data infrastructure good enough for expansion beyond a few geofenced areas.

## Broad Winners

- Ride-hailing and delivery platforms.
- Autonomous vehicle operators.
- OEMs producing AV-ready vehicles at scale.
- Edge compute providers and AI silicon suppliers.
- Camera, lidar, radar, and sensor suppliers, although commoditisation risk is high.
- Fleet management, maintenance, charging, and depot infrastructure providers.
- Mapping, simulation, and autonomy software/tooling providers.
- Insurers or data providers that can price AV fleet risk accurately.

## Specific Winners To Investigate

### Uber

Potentially one of the cleanest public-market beneficiaries if Uber becomes the demand aggregation layer for robotaxis without needing to own all fleet capex. Uber already has consumers, pricing, dispatch, payments, maps, merchants, and driver/delivery marketplace infrastructure.

The key question: does Uber capture the customer relationship, or do AV operators go direct?

Things to watch:
- Partnerships with Waymo, Tesla, BYD, Motional, Baidu, or other AV providers.
- Autonomous ride volume disclosed by Uber.
- Take-rate on autonomous rides versus human-driver rides.
- Whether Uber avoids heavy balance-sheet fleet ownership.
- Evidence that robotaxi supply lowers consumer prices and expands trip frequency.

### Alphabet / Waymo

Waymo is one of the most credible AV operators. Alphabet gives exposure, but Waymo is still only a small part of the overall business.

Things to watch:
- Weekly paid rides.
- Number of operating cities.
- Cost per mile.
- Expansion speed outside highly mapped/geofenced areas.
- Whether Waymo remains vertically integrated or partners broadly with platforms like Uber.

### Tesla

Tesla has enormous upside if it can deliver scalable unsupervised autonomy using its existing fleet and manufacturing base. The risk is that timelines and technical claims have historically run ahead of reality.

Things to watch:
- Unsupervised FSD approvals.
- True driverless miles without safety drivers.
- Robotaxi revenue disclosure.
- Safety data per million miles.
- Regulatory treatment after incidents.

### BYD / Chinese OEMs

Chinese OEMs may become important if AV hardware becomes a scale manufacturing game. BYD’s manufacturing capability and EV cost structure are relevant, but autonomy stack ownership matters.

Things to watch:
- Partnerships with autonomy providers.
- Robotaxi fleet deployments in China or export markets.
- Unit economics of AV-ready EVs.

### Nvidia

Nvidia benefits if AVs require heavy training and inference compute. It may be a broad picks-and-shovels winner regardless of which AV operator wins.

Things to watch:
- Automotive segment revenue.
- Design wins for DRIVE platforms.
- Edge inference requirements per vehicle.
- Competition from custom silicon.

### Sensor Suppliers

Potential winners include lidar/radar/camera suppliers, but this basket has higher risk because hardware can commoditise and architecture choices remain unsettled.

Things to watch:
- Which sensor stack wins: camera-only, lidar-heavy, radar fusion, or hybrid.
- Gross margins and pricing pressure.
- OEM design wins that convert to volume production.

## What To Monitor

- Robotaxi rides per week by city.
- Number of operating cities and speed of geographic expansion.
- Cost per mile versus human-driven Uber/taxi services.
- Safety incidents per million miles.
- Regulatory approvals, suspensions, or restrictions.
- Insurance cost per AV mile.
- Fleet utilisation and vehicle uptime.
- Consumer repeat usage and wait times.
- Partnerships between ride-hailing platforms and AV operators.
- Evidence of households reducing car ownership.
- Delivery/logistics AV deployments.
- Capex intensity: who owns the vehicles, depots, charging, and maintenance burden?

## Risks / Bear Case

- AVs remain technically brittle outside limited geofenced areas.
- Regulation slows rollout after safety incidents.
- Unit economics disappoint due to vehicle capex, maintenance, cleaning, remote support, insurance, or low utilisation.
- Consumers use robotaxis occasionally but do not abandon car ownership.
- OEMs and AV operators go direct, reducing ride-hailing platform economics.
- Sensor and hardware suppliers face brutal commoditisation.
- Public backlash or labour politics slows adoption.

## Investment Framing

This thesis should probably be tracked in stages:

1. **Proof of technical reliability** — robotaxis operate safely and repeatedly in real cities.
2. **Proof of scaling** — expansion into many cities without bespoke manual effort exploding.
3. **Proof of unit economics** — cost per mile falls below human-driven ride-hailing.
4. **Proof of behavioural change** — consumers take more trips and reduce car ownership.

The biggest upside comes if all four happen. If only the first two happen, the winners may be narrow AV operators and suppliers rather than a broad transport transformation.
