# Trait: Pricing Power / Durable Moat

**What we're looking for:**
A company that can lift prices ahead of inflation, consistently, without losing customers or volume,
because the customer has nowhere better to go. These companies have a **structural, proven competitive advantage** 
that protects the business's high returns from being competed away. High returns attract competitors 
who erode pricing power; a *moat* is whatever stops that from happening. This trait asks **why** 
the returns persist. 

* A history of **price increases at or above inflation** with **volume and market share holding**.
* **High and stable (or rising) gross margins** — the financial signature of pricing power.
* Demand that is **relatively price-inelastic** — because of brand, necessity, criticality, or a
  small-ticket-but-essential role in the customer's life.

**Where pricing power comes from:**
| Source | What it is | The tell |
|---|---|---|
| **Intangible assets** | Brands that command a price premium, patents, regulatory licences/approvals | Customers pay more for the same underlying product; rivals are legally or perceptually locked out |
| **Switching costs** | Cost, risk, or hassle of leaving locks customers in | Low churn, long contracts, deep integration, certification/retraining barriers |
| **Network effects** | Each new user makes the product more valuable to the others | The leader's lead compounds; a subscale rival can't match the utility |
| **Cost advantage** | Structurally lower costs — scale, process, location, unique assets | It can underprice rivals *and still* out-earn them; lowest-cost producer |

**Direction matters most:**
A moat is not static — the decisive question is whether it is **widening, stable, or narrowing**.
The best moat is one that widens over time. Weigh the threats honestly: technological disruption,
deregulation, patent expiry, changing consumer habits, or a deep-pocketed new entrant. A wide moat
visibly under attack scores worse than a narrower one that is quietly getting wider.

**Key questions:**

* Can the company raise prices ahead of inflation without losing volume or share? What's the evidence?
* Which structural source(s) above produce the pricing power — and is each backed by concrete evidence?
* Does the advantage rest on weak competitors who could wake up? 
* Is the moat widening, holding, or narrowing — and what are the live threats to it?

**Scoring guidance:**

* **0.70–1.00** A clear, structural moat from one or more durable sources; demonstrated pricing power
  (raises prices without losing customers; high, stable gross margins); the moat is stable or
  widening and hard and expensive to attack.
* **0.40–0.69** A real but **narrow or uncertain** moat — some advantage and some pricing power, but
  vulnerable to a determined competitor, slowly eroding, or dependent on conditions that could
  change; evidence is mixed.
* **0.00–0.39** No durable moat — competes largely on price, little or no pricing power, commoditised
  economics, or a moat visibly eroding under disruption.

**Documentation:**

* The moat source(s) from the table that apply, with concrete evidence for each
* Pricing-power evidence: price increases versus inflation and the volume/share response; gross-margin
  level and trend — with source
* Direction — widening / stable / narrowing — with evidence, and the main live threats to the moat
* The reason for the score — why the moat is judged structural and durable (or narrow / absent)
