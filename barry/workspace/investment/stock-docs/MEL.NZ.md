# MEL.NZ — Meridian Energy Limited

## Business Overview

Meridian Energy is one of New Zealand's largest electricity gentailers. It generates roughly 30% of the country's electricity and sells energy to about 17% of households and businesses through Meridian and Powershop. The profit engine is mostly renewable generation — large hydro plus wind — sold into wholesale, industrial and retail channels; retail provides customer margin and demand matching, while the wholesale book carries hydrology, wind, hedge and regulatory risk.

FY25 was a stress-test year rather than a normal year: two consecutive record-low hydro inflow periods, low wind and constrained gas availability drove expensive demand-response and hedge actions. Operating cash flow fell to NZ$318m, EBITDAF fell 32% to NZ$611m, underlying net profit fell to NZ$56m, and reported NPAT was a NZ$452m loss, mainly due to the changed accounting treatment/fair-value impact of NZAS contracts and hedges. The board still declared total FY25 ordinary dividends of NZ$0.21/share, citing a BBB+/Stable credit profile and balance-sheet capacity to invest.

## Trait Assessment

### Earnings Quality / Consistent, Predictable Profits — 0.45 / 0.75

Meridian has real earning assets and positive operating profit, but reported earnings are not clean or predictable enough for a high score. FY25 swung to a NZ$452m reported loss from NZ$429m profit in FY24; underlying net profit was only NZ$56m after severe hydro/wind/gas conditions and NZAS/hedge accounting changes. Yahoo-derived annual data shows one loss year in the four-year window, high net-income growth dispersion, but cumulative FCF/net-income conversion of 1.79x. Interpretation: cash generation is real, but earnings are weather-, hydro-, hedge- and fair-value-sensitive, so the consistency strand is weak. Confidence would firm up with a full 10-year normalised EBITDAF/underlying-NPAT bridge across hydrology cycles.

### Free Cash Flow Generation — 0.45 / 0.75

The business generates free cash, but not at a classic cash-cow level. FY25 operating cash flow was NZ$318m and capex about NZ$184m, leaving NZ$134m FCF; dividends paid were NZ$387m, so the cash payout was not covered in that stress year. Scripted Yahoo metrics show FCF margins of 2.8% in FY25 and about 5.8% average over four years, with a falling latest trend. Interpretation: the hydro asset base produces cash, but ongoing renewable build-out, maintenance capex and dry-year insurance costs mean the cash is not fully surplus. Confidence would improve with management's maintenance-vs-growth capex split and a normal-hydrology FCF bridge.

### Fundamental Stability / Secular Durability — 0.65 / 0.80

Electricity demand is durable, and Meridian owns hard-to-replicate renewable assets in a country needing substantially more generation. The 2025 report says New Zealand may need 50% to almost 100% more generation by 2050; Meridian has 5.9GW/13.9TWh of development options. The FY25 weakness appears cyclical/weather and fuel-system related rather than secular demand decline. The offset is genuine: hydro inflow volatility, gas scarcity, wholesale-price politics and regulation can impair realised margins for long periods. Overall, the asset base is durable but cash flows are not as stable as a regulated utility.

### Pricing Power / Durable Moat — 0.58 / 0.70

Meridian's moat comes from scale, scarce renewable hydro/wind assets, retail load, generation flexibility and a renewable brand/REC product. It reports 250 companies buying more than 1,600GWh of RECs in FY25, up 94% year on year. But electricity is partly commoditised and politically sensitive; the FY25 report notes proposed Electricity Authority obligations around hedge sales and sector scrutiny after dry-year price spikes. Interpretation: Meridian has a real asset/scale moat, but pricing power is capped by regulation, customer affordability and weather-driven market design pressure. Confidence would firm with market-share and churn/contract-margin trend data.

### Capital Allocation — 0.62 / 0.65

Management is prioritising renewable development, operational restoration, storage and shareholder returns. Ruakākā BESS was completed inside its NZ$186m capital envelope; Harapaki became fully operational; consents/JVs progressed across wind, solar and batteries. The board maintained the dividend while preserving BBB+/Stable and retained flexibility to review dividends in future severe droughts. The caveat is payout discipline: FY25 dividends exceeded FCF materially, and large development capex creates execution risk. Confidence is only moderate because the return on the growth pipeline is not yet fully observable.

### Returns on Capital — 0.35 / 0.75

Returns are low on the current capital base. Scripted Yahoo metrics show latest ROIC of -3.7% in FY25 and a four-year average around 2.7%; even the stronger FY24 was about 5.0% ROIC. A capital-intensive renewable utility can deserve a lower hurdle than a software/brand compounder, but these returns are still not high relative to likely cost of capital. Revalued generation assets also increase the denominator. This is the main reason Meridian does not score like a wonderful compounder.

### Conservative Debt / Financial Strength — 0.62 / 0.75

The balance sheet is acceptable for a capital-intensive gentailer, not pristine. Meridian disclosed FY25 net debt of NZ$1.505b, net debt/EBITDAF of 2.5x, total borrowings of NZ$1.569b, total facilities of NZ$2.161b and NZ$658m undrawn, with BBB+/Stable credit rating. The next capital-market maturity is September 2028 after a September 2025 green bond issue. That is manageable and well-funded, but FY25's EBITDAF drop shows leverage can move quickly when hydrology turns. Confidence would improve with the latest post-2025 maturity ladder and covenant headroom.

### Key-Person Risk — 0.82 / 0.75

The thesis is institutional, not founder-dependent. Neal Barclay retired on 30 June 2025 and was replaced by former CFO Mike Roan from 1 July 2025. Meridian's value is embedded in physical assets, licences/consents, operational systems, retail brands and sector position rather than one individual. The score is high, though not perfect, because a new CEO still needs to execute a large renewables pipeline and manage a politically sensitive market.

### Incentive Alignment — 0.68 / 0.75

The remuneration system is reasonably aligned: FY25 STI was 60% financial performance and 40% strategic scorecard, and no STI was paid because the financial threshold was missed despite 75% scorecard achievement. LTI hurdles include absolute TSR above cost of equity plus 1% and relative TSR against the S&P/NZX50. Positives are real performance linkage and transparency. Offsets: total remuneration targets the upper quartile for executives; the majority government shareholding and 10% ownership cap reduce takeover discipline; clawbacks are limited. Overall, incentives are decent but not owner-perfect.

**Why the other strategies lost:** Wonderful-and-fair fails mainly on returns on capital and earnings predictability; the runway exists, but current returns are too low for a quality-compounder score. Freeroll lacks a clear catalyst and the downside floor is not tight enough: price is ~1.7x book/tangible book and dividend yield is only ~3.8%, not a hard floor. Rule-breakers has a renewable-growth theme and a top-dog position, but Meridian is a mature capital-intensive utility in an established electricity market, not a high-growth emerging-category disruptor.

## Overall

Meridian is a durable renewable infrastructure owner with a credible growth pipeline, strong strategic relevance to New Zealand electrification and a survivable balance sheet. The case is income-plus-modest-growth, not high-quality compounding. The main detractors are low returns on capital, weather/hedge-driven earnings volatility, dividend coverage stress in bad years, and regulatory/political pressure when power prices spike. I would keep it watched, but at NZ$5.73 the valuation leaves only a modest forward return unless normal earnings recover and the market continues to award a premium multiple.

## Thesis Connections

Potential FIT: New Zealand electrification, renewable generation build-out, grid-scale storage and corporate decarbonisation demand. Meridian is a direct beneficiary if power demand rises and new renewable capacity earns adequate returns. The thesis is weaker if regulatory intervention caps generator returns or if dry-year volatility forces repeated expensive insurance/demand-response actions.

## Watch

- FY26 normal-hydrology recovery: EBITDAF, underlying NPAT, OCF and FCF versus FY25 trough.
- Dividend coverage: whether NZ$0.21/share remains covered by normalised FCF rather than balance-sheet capacity.
- Regulatory changes from the Electricity Authority and government pressure on gentailer hedge/market obligations.
- Execution and returns on Ruakākā solar/BESS, Te Rahui solar JV, Te Rere Hau repowering and the 5.9GW development pipeline.
- Net debt/EBITDAF and credit-rating headroom through the renewable capex cycle.
- Mike Roan's first full year as CEO: capital discipline, project delivery and communication quality.
