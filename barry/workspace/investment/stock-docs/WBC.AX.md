# WBC.AX — Westpac Banking Corporation

## Business Overview

Westpac is one of Australia’s four major banks, earning most of its profit from the spread between funding costs and interest received on Australian and New Zealand housing, business and institutional loans, plus fees and markets income. At 31 March 2026 it held 20% of Australian housing credit, 17% of business credit, 21% of household deposits and 18% of business deposits. Gross loans were A$890.3bn and customer deposits A$745.2bn. In the June quarter, deposits and lending each grew 2%, with lending growth broad-based across business, institutional and housing; the A$15.4bn RAMS mortgage portfolio was sold on 1 August 2026, reducing complexity and adding 23bp to CET1.[4][5] The franchise is mature, regulated and systemically important; returns depend chiefly on loan and deposit growth, net interest margin (NIM), credit losses, operating efficiency and capital discipline.

## Trait Assessment

### Earnings Quality / Consistent, Predictable Profits — 0.72 (confidence 0.90)

Westpac remained profitable through the pandemic and subsequent rate cycle. Statutory NPAT was A$5.46bn in FY2021, A$5.69bn in FY2022, A$7.20bn in FY2023, A$6.99bn in FY2024 and A$6.92bn in FY2025; 1H26 NPAT rose 3% year on year to A$3.41bn. Unaudited 3Q26 statutory NPAT was A$1.8bn, up 3% on the 1H26 quarterly average; NPAT excluding notable items was also A$1.8bn, up 2%, with no notable items in the quarter.[4][5] This is dependable but not smooth compounding: earnings still move with margins, provisions and remediation costs. Conventional operating cash flow and FCF are not meaningful quality checks for a deposit-taking bank because changes in loans, deposits and financial assets dominate the cash-flow statement. Regulatory capital generation, credit losses and dividends are better cash-realisation proxies.

### Free Cash Flow Generation — 0.62 (confidence 0.72)

For a bank, reported FCF is economically misleading: customer deposits are operating funding and loan growth consumes cash, so ordinary industrial FCF margins and capex/OCF ratios do not describe distributable cash. The closest owner-cash proxy is sustainable earnings after retaining enough capital for loan growth and regulatory buffers. Westpac paid A$1.53 per share in FY2025 and maintained a 77-cent interim dividend in 1H26; the adjusted payout ratio was 75.6%, while CET1 remained 12.4%, A$2.7bn above its 11.25% target.[1][2] This supports meaningful distributability, but the high payout leaves less self-funded growth capacity and distributions remain subject to APRA capital requirements. Confidence is irreducible under the generic FCF rubric; revisit after FY2026 results provide a full-year capital-generation bridge and dividend outcome.

### Fundamental Stability / Secular Durability — 0.78 (confidence 0.90)

The core deposit-and-lending franchise is stable rather than structurally declining. Over the year to March 2026, loans and deposits each grew 7%; in 3Q26, deposits and lending each added a further 2%, including 4% growth in business lending, 3% in institutional and 2% in housing.[1][4] Credit quality remains sound but stopped improving: stressed exposures rose 3bp in the quarter to 1.19% of total committed exposure, while impairment charges remained 10bp of average gross loans and provisions above the base-case expected loss increased to A$2.0bn.[4][5] The main structural threats are digital competitors, commoditised mortgages, cyber/operational risk and the cost of legacy-system simplification. None is presently eroding the franchise; 3Q26 NIM was stable at 1.89%, core NIM improved 1bp from 2Q26 to 1.78%, and UNITE remained on track.[4]

### Pricing Power / Durable Moat — 0.66 (confidence 0.82)

Westpac’s moat comes from scale, a large low-cost deposit base, trusted brands, nationwide distribution, regulatory barriers and customer switching friction. Stable 20–21% shares in housing credit and household deposits demonstrate franchise durability.[1] Pricing power is nevertheless constrained by intense major-bank and broker-channel competition: core NIM declined 2bp year on year to 1.78%, partly because of mortgage competition and deposit pricing.[1] This is a defensible franchise, not an unconstrained price setter.

### Capital Allocation — 0.76 (confidence 0.91)

Management is returning surplus capital while preserving a clear buffer. FY2025 ordinary dividends were A$1.53 per share, up from A$1.18 in FY2021, and the adjusted payout ratio was 75%. Westpac also completed capital returns in prior periods, including a 2024 special dividend and buyback activity. CET1 was 12.1% at June 2026 after the interim dividend and loan-driven RWA growth, still above the 11.25% target; the August RAMS sale added a further 23bp.[4][5] The allocation case is sound for a mature bank, though UNITE's spending and execution risk must ultimately produce lower complexity and costs rather than become a recurring transformation burden.

### High, Durable Returns on Capital — 0.55 (confidence 0.94)

ROTE is the appropriate metric because regulatory equity is the productive capital for a bank. Statutory ROTE ranged from 8.82% to 11.39% over FY2021–FY2025; excluding notable items it stayed between 10.58% and 11.67%. In 1H26 adjusted ROTE was 11.01%.[1][2] Returns are stable and above Westpac’s cost of equity only modestly, but fall short of the roughly 15% “strong bank” anchor. Leverage is inherent to banking, so ROTE is read alongside capital adequacy rather than treated as an unqualified high-return signal. Recent returns are flat, not improving materially.

### Conservative Debt / Financial Strength — 0.84 (confidence 0.94)

Bank debt ratios are not comparable with industrial companies; regulatory capital, liquidity and funding are decisive. At June 2026 CET1 was 12.1% versus the 11.25% target, quarterly-average LCR was 134%, NSFR 111%, and customer deposits funded 84.1% of net loans.[4][5] The RAMS sale subsequently added 23bp to CET1. Wholesale funding remains necessary and stressed exposures edged up to 1.19%, but current capital, liquidity and provision buffers remain strong. No solvency, hidden-liability, accounting-integrity, abusive-control or imminent business-model disqualifier was identified.

### Key-Person Risk — 0.88 (confidence 0.86)

CEO Anthony Miller matters to UNITE execution, but Westpac’s value resides mainly in regulated licences, brands, deposits, systems and a broad executive bench rather than any individual. The board-appointed leadership structure and institutionalised risk controls provide succession depth; there is no founder control or super-voting structure. A CEO departure could delay transformation, but would not break the deposit and lending franchise.[2]

### Incentive Alignment — 0.75 (confidence 0.88)

The FY2025 framework combines short-term scorecards with long-term equity. Half of STVR is deferred into equity for up to two years; LTVR uses four-year assessment periods, relative TSR performance rights and risk-culture gates, with malus and clawback available. The CEO holding requirement rose to three times fixed remuneration from October 2025. Financial, customer, risk and strategic metrics reduce single-metric gaming.[2] Soft spots remain: annual STVR is material, part of LTVR is service-based restricted rights, and ownership largely accumulates through remuneration rather than open-market buying.

Cash-cow is the best fit because Westpac is a mature, profitable franchise returning most earnings. Wonderful-and-fair loses on only moderate ROTE and limited high-return reinvestment runway. Rule-breakers loses on mature-industry and low-growth gates. Freeroll lacks both a cheap floor—the shares trade around 1.76× book and 18× trailing earnings—and a specific time-bound rerating catalyst.[1][3]

## Overall

Westpac is a financially strong, durable income franchise with broad-based lending momentum, sound credit quality and a well-covered dividend. The June quarter showed modest operating improvement: NPAT, revenue and pre-provision profit all rose versus the first-half quarterly average, NIM was stable, and adjusted ROTE increased to 11.4%.[4][5] Its weaknesses are equally clear: only moderate returns, competitive margin pressure, a high payout that limits retained compounding, and transformation execution risk. It fits a cash-cow portfolio, but the current valuation offers little margin of safety.

## Valuation

The exit-multiple model uses earnings because conventional bank FCF is not meaningful and banks are commonly valued on P/E and book value. The A$2.03 current EPS anchor matches trailing earnings and is close to the 1H26 annualised statutory run-rate of about A$2.00 per share. The model assumes 2% aggregate earnings growth, 0.2% annual net buybacks, a 4.21% dividend yield, five years and a 14× exit P/E. It produces a 1.93% annualised ROI: roughly +2.00% from business growth, +0.20% from buybacks and +4.75% from income, offset by −4.91% annualised multiple contraction. The 14× exit multiple is below the current 18.0× entry multiple and still generous for an approximately 11% ROTE bank, so the result does not rely on expansion.[1][3] The agent score is 0.45: franchise quality and income are acceptable, but the entry valuation compresses prospective return.

## Watch

- FY2026 full-year capital-generation bridge, payout and CET1 after distributions; this is the next evidence for the bank-adjusted FCF assessment.
- UNITE milestones, spend and measurable reduction in the expense-to-income ratio.
- Core NIM and mortgage/deposit market share: persistent margin erosion would weaken the moat assessment.
- Stressed exposures, mortgage arrears and impairment charges as economic conditions change.
- Confirm whether the 3Q26 increase in stressed exposures and more conservative downside overlays persist or reverse at the full-year result.
- ROTE progression toward management’s medium-term ambitions; sustained results below cost of equity would impair the thesis.
