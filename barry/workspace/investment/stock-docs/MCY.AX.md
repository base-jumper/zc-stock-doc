# MCY.AX — Mercury NZ Limited

## Business Overview

Mercury NZ is an integrated New Zealand electricity generator and retailer. Its generation assets are 100% renewable — hydro, geothermal and wind — and it also sells electricity, gas, broadband and mobile services to retail customers. The NZ Government has a legislated minimum 51% shareholding, and the stock is listed on the NZX and ASX. Source quality is good for the operating facts: FY25 and HY26 company announcements, with Yahoo Finance used for market price and historical financial statements.

The business is mainly an infrastructure-style renewable generator/retailer: earnings are driven by hydrology and wind resource, wholesale power prices, plant availability, retail customer numbers, gas/firming constraints, operating costs, and the return Mercury earns on major renewable development capex.

HY26 was much stronger than the weak FY25 base: EBITDAF was NZ$537m, up 28% on HY25, supported by above-average hydro generation and lower operating costs. Full-year FY26 EBITDAF guidance remained NZ$1.0b. Mercury is simultaneously reinvesting heavily: the company said about NZ$270m, or half HY26 earnings, was reinvested into new and existing generation assets.

## Trait Assessment

### Earnings Quality / Consistent, Predictable Profits — 0.55 / confidence 0.75

Mercury is profitable and cash-generative at the EBITDAF level, but statutory earnings are not smooth enough to call this a high-quality predictable profit stream. Yahoo annual data shows net income of NZ$469m in FY22, NZ$103m in FY23, NZ$290m in FY24 and only NZ$1m in FY25; the FY25 release attributes the drop mainly to lower EBITDAF and unrealised gains/losses on unhedged electricity derivatives. Cumulative FCF/net income over Yahoo's four-year window was about 0.91x, so cash backing is not the core issue; volatility is.

Interpretation: the underlying asset base is durable, but hydrology, wholesale prices, derivatives and capital intensity make accounting profit lumpy. Confidence would firm up with a full 10-year EBITDAF/NPAT bridge from the annual reports.

### Free Cash Flow Generation — 0.48 / confidence 0.65

On headline free cash flow after all capex, Mercury is not an attractive cash cow today. Yahoo shows FCF of NZ$213m, NZ$281m, NZ$278m and only NZ$16m across FY22-FY25, with FY25 FCF margin around 0.5%. The FY25 release explains the pressure: stay-in-business capex was NZ$138m, but growth capex rose to NZ$347m as Kaiwera Downs 2 and Kaiwaikawe entered construction. HY26 continued the pattern, with half of HY26 earnings reinvested.

Interpretation: maintenance owner earnings are materially better than headline FCF because growth capex is discretionary and tied to the buildout, but current distributable free cash is thin. This is an infrastructure reinvestment phase, not a pure harvest phase. Confidence is moderate because Yahoo does not split growth vs maintenance capex cleanly outside management disclosures.

### Fundamental Stability / Secular Durability — 0.72 / confidence 0.80

The core demand setup is durable: electricity demand should be supported by electrification, and Mercury is adding renewable generation into a market that needs firm, low-carbon supply. HY26 management reiterated a plan to add 3.5TWh of new generation by 2030 and said major renewable developments were progressing on budget and on time. Retail connections rose 5% to 906,000 in FY25, with 38% of customers taking two or more products.

The caveat is not secular demand but variability: FY25 generation was 7,906GWh, down 10%, with hydro down 17% in the fourth-lowest Waikato year since 1980, wind down 6%, and geothermal down 2% due to planned outages. That is cyclical/resource variability rather than a melting-ice-cube demand problem.

### Pricing Power / Durable Moat — 0.62 / confidence 0.70

Mercury's moat is asset-based: renewable generation sites, scale, an integrated gen-retail book, and a pipeline of consented/advanced projects are hard to replicate quickly. The company is investing in Ngā Tamariki geothermal expansion, Kaiwera Downs 2, Kaiwaikawe, hydro refurbishments and demand flexibility such as smart hot-water control.

Pricing power is not clean. Electricity is politically sensitive, retail competition is active, and Mercury itself noted customer affordability initiatives and sector-confidence work. Gross margin and operating margin have been volatile in Yahoo data. The moat is real, but not the kind of unregulated pricing power that earns a top score.

### Capital Allocation — 0.58 / confidence 0.65

Management is reinvesting aggressively where the country needs capacity: NZ$1b across three major renewable builds, a completed Karāpiro hydro upgrade, and a planned NZ$590m hydro refurbishment programme over the next decade. FY26 guidance points to NZ$1.0b EBITDAF, NZ$600m growth capex and NZ$150m stay-in-business capex.

The tension is the dividend. FY25 ordinary dividends rose 3% to 24.0cps and FY26 guidance is 25.0cps, but FY25 headline FCF was only NZ$16m after growth capex and the DRP is being used. That can be rational while building long-life assets, but it means capital allocation is not yet a clean surplus-cash-return story.

### High, Durable Returns on Capital — 0.35 / confidence 0.75

Returns are modest. Yahoo-derived ROIC was about 7.8% in FY22, 2.6% in FY23, 5.7% in FY24 and 1.3% in FY25, with a four-year average around 4.3%. For a utility this is not automatically disastrous — regulated/contractual infrastructure earns lower returns than software — but it is not a high-return compounder fingerprint, and FY25 was weak.

Interpretation: Mercury may earn acceptable infrastructure returns over time if the new renewable projects hit target economics, but the current reported return base is low and falling.

### Conservative Debt / Financial Strength — 0.48 / confidence 0.70

Debt is acceptable for a renewable utility but not conservative. Yahoo/scripting shows TTM net debt/EBITDA around 3.0x, while the FY25 annual snapshot showed net debt/EBITDA around 4.65x and EBIT interest cover only around 1.0x. FY26 management said the balance sheet remains strong with capital headroom and prudent risk settings, but the numbers are stretched during the build phase.

For an infrastructure utility with durable assets and government majority ownership, this is survivable. It is still a real constraint: elevated capex plus dividends plus hydrology volatility leave less room for error.

### Key-Person Risk — 0.82 / confidence 0.70

The thesis is not dependent on one person. Mercury's value sits in generation assets, development pipeline, retail systems, customer book, grid/market position and institutional governance. CEO Stew Hamilton matters, but the business does not appear founder- or star-person dependent. Confidence would improve with a deeper review of succession disclosures.

### Incentive Alignment — 0.55 / confidence 0.55

Alignment looks mixed. HY26 disclosed that Mercury revised executive remuneration to incentivise strategic performance and long-term shareholder value. The Government's 51% minimum stake adds stability but also introduces policy objectives alongside minority-shareholder returns. Yahoo flags high shareholder-rights risk, and I have not yet reviewed the full remuneration report. This is directionally acceptable but under-researched.

### Strategy fit and why the others lost

The scorer showed a near-tie between cash-cow and wonderful-and-fair. Cash-cow wins because Mercury is best framed as a mature infrastructure asset with income plus modest growth, while the wonderful-and-fair case is capped by low reported ROIC and only moderate reinvestment economics. It is not a classic high-return compounder yet; it would need the current renewable buildout to prove high incremental returns.

Rule-breakers loses because this is a mature utility, not an emerging category winner with explosive organic growth. Freeroll loses because there is no obvious hard floor close to price or discrete re-rating catalyst; the setup is a yield/infrastructure reinvestment story rather than a near-term asymmetry.

## Overall

Mercury is a durable renewable infrastructure business in a structurally useful place, but it is only a borderline cash-cow today. The asset base, government-backed stability, dividend record and renewable buildout are attractive. The weaknesses are equally clear: low reported returns on capital, stretched build-phase leverage, hydrology volatility, and weak headline FCF after growth capex.

My current view: worth watching, not obviously attractive at the ASX price. The thesis improves if the FY26 EBITDAF recovery lands, the new generation projects commission on time/budget, and growth capex starts converting into per-share owner earnings without pushing leverage higher.

## Valuation

I used the cash-cow exit-multiple method on a maintenance-owner-earnings basis, in NZD. The ASX price of A$5.65 was converted at AUD/NZD 1.22, giving about NZ$6.89 per share. I used normalised owner earnings/FCF of NZ$0.34 per share, based on FY26 EBITDAF guidance of NZ$1.0b less cash interest, tax and stay-in-business capex, rather than depressed FY25 headline FCF after growth capex. The model assumes 5 years, 3% annual growth, 0.5% annual dilution/DRP leakage, a 17x exit multiple, and a 74% payout ratio approximating the 25cps FY26 dividend guidance.

That gives an estimated annualized ROI of about 3.0%. Return attribution is mostly dividend income, partly offset by expected multiple compression from the current ~20x owner-earnings entry multiple to 17x. This is not enough compensation for the leverage/build risk unless confidence in the new assets' returns improves.

Agent score: 0.45. Quality is real but not exceptional; valuation is not compelling.

## Thesis Connections

FIT connection is moderate: Mercury is directly exposed to electrification, renewable generation buildout, grid flexibility and decarbonisation of industrial energy demand. It is a picks-and-shovels renewable infrastructure participant rather than a high-upside technology winner.

## Watch

- FY26 result versus NZ$1.0b EBITDAF guidance; separate hydrology luck from sustainable earnings improvement.
- Commissioning of Ngā Tamariki fifth unit, Kaiwera Downs 2 and Kaiwaikawe; monitor budget/timing slippage and actual generation contribution.
- Net debt/EBITDA and interest cover as growth capex peaks.
- Dividend coverage from maintenance owner earnings, not just DRP-supported headline payout.
- Evidence that new renewable projects earn acceptable incremental returns on capital.
- Regulatory/political pressure on electricity prices and retail margins in New Zealand.
- Full FY26 annual report remuneration detail to firm up incentive-alignment confidence.
