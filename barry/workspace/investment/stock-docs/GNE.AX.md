# GNE.AX — Genesis Energy Limited

## Business Overview

Genesis Energy is a New Zealand gentailer: it generates electricity, sells electricity/gas/LPG to retail and commercial customers, and owns 46% of the Kupe gas field. The portfolio combines Huntly thermal/flexible generation, hydro schemes, solar/renewable PPAs and retail brands now being consolidated under Genesis. FY25 reported EBITDAF was NZ$454m, normalised EBITDAF was NZ$470m and NPAT was NZ$169m; the company guides toward mid-to-upper NZ$500m EBITDAF by FY28, assuming average hydro and no major plant/regulatory shocks.

The core economic engine is not high-growth compounding. It is a mature utility cash stream plus optional upside from flexible generation scarcity, Huntly Firming Options, solar build-out, battery storage and retail simplification. That makes **cash-cow** the best fit: covered yield, positive FCF and mature infrastructure value, but with enough leverage, regulatory exposure and commodity/weather volatility to keep the score moderate rather than clean.

Universal disqualifiers checked: no evidence found of fraud, insolvency, value-leaking control, or a business-model kill-shot. The material risks are normal utility risks — debt, fuel availability, carbon/regulatory pressure, hydrology and execution — not disqualifiers.

## Trait Assessment

### Earnings Quality / Consistent, Predictable Profits — 0.64 / confidence 0.78

Genesis has stayed profitable across the last four reported years: FY22 NPAT NZ$222m, FY23 NZ$196m, FY24 NZ$131m and FY25 NZ$169m, with FY25 normalised EBITDAF NZ$470m. That is real earning power, but not especially smooth: profitability moves with hydrology, gas supply, wholesale volatility, revaluations and generation mix. FY25 itself was described as dynamic, with winter 2024 stress, gas constraints and unusually variable quarters.

Cash backing is acceptable: FY22–FY25 cumulative free cash flow was roughly NZ$968m versus cumulative net income of roughly NZ$718m on Yahoo annual cash-flow/income data. The caveat is that FCF is lumpy and capex/investment requirements are rising under Gen35. Confidence would improve with a 10-year filing series and a cleaner split between maintenance and growth capex.

### Free Cash Flow Generation — 0.66 / confidence 0.80

Genesis generated positive FCF in each of FY22–FY25: NZ$168m, NZ$335m, NZ$288m and NZ$177m respectively. Yahoo's current data also shows operating cash flow around NZ$449m and FCF around NZ$196m. That supports the dividend and makes the cash-cow framing legitimate.

The weakness is capital intensity. FY25 capex was about NZ$135m and investing cash flow was NZ$240m as solar, battery, technology and portfolio work ramp. Depreciation was NZ$239m, so reported capex alone is below D&A, but the business is not capital-light; large energy assets need reinvestment, and Gen35 adds growth spend. The cash is real, but not “software-like” free.

### Fundamental Stability / Secular Durability — 0.70 / confidence 0.76

Electricity demand, firming capacity and retail energy supply are durable needs. Genesis has more than 520,000 customers and geographically diverse generation assets. Huntly's flexible capacity has become more strategically valuable as New Zealand adds intermittent renewables and faces gas/hydro/wind variability; the company signed 10-year Huntly Firming Option agreements after balance date, subject to Commerce Commission review.

The main durability risk is transition complexity. Kupe production is declining, domestic gas supply is constrained, coal use faces political/carbon pressure, and wholesale market reform is live after winter 2024. Genesis is responding with solar, batteries, biomass investigation and flexible products, but execution and regulation matter. Confidence would improve after the HFO agreements are fully approved/operational and after FY26 shows whether FY28 EBITDAF guidance is tracking.

### Pricing Power / Durable Moat — 0.57 / confidence 0.68

Genesis has some moat characteristics: scale, generation/retail integration, scarce firming assets, hydro assets, Huntly's role in system security, and a large customer base. The firming value of Huntly may strengthen as intermittent renewable penetration rises.

Retail electricity remains competitive, and Genesis explicitly notes healthy retail competition plus competition for renewable development sites, grid connection and engineering resources. Gross margins are not structurally high, and pricing is constrained by regulation, competition and public/political scrutiny. This is a narrow infrastructure moat, not a wide pricing-power franchise. Confidence would improve with customer churn, retail gross-margin and contract book detail.

### Capital Allocation — 0.61 / confidence 0.72

Management is pursuing a balanced yield-plus-growth strategy: maintaining a BBB+ credit rating, paying a high imputed dividend, investing in solar/battery/flexible generation, simplifying retail brands, and acquiring the remaining Ecotricity stake and a majority ChargeNet position. The strategy is coherent: defend the cash stream while building renewable and flexibility options.

But the record is not yet proven. Share count has crept up through dividend reinvestment, leverage is meaningful, and recent acquisitions/projects need to prove returns. FY25 dividends paid were NZ$115.8m versus FCF of NZ$176.6m, covered but not with a huge buffer after growth investment. Capital allocation gets a moderate positive score, not a high one.

### High, Durable Returns on Capital — 0.43 / confidence 0.86

This is the main quality drag. The scorer data shows ROIC of roughly 5.0% in FY25, 4.5% in FY24, 6.6% in FY23 and 6.9% in FY22; ROCE ranged roughly 5.5%–8.1%. These are utility/infrastructure-style returns, not high compounder returns, and likely only modestly above cost of capital.

ROE is also modest: Yahoo shows TTM ROE around 6.4%, and annual ROE was around 5.7% in FY25. Genesis may create value through scarcity value and better utilisation of existing flexible assets, but the current return profile is average.

### Conservative Debt / Financial Strength — 0.57 / confidence 0.84

Debt is manageable for a utility but not conservative in an absolute sense. FY25 net debt was around NZ$1.41b, net debt/EBITDA about 2.6x on annual figures and 3.05x on Yahoo's current TTM snapshot. FY25 EBIT interest cover was about 4.2x; EBITDA interest cover about 7.6x. Borrowings are unsecured and the Board states commitment to a long-term BBB+ credit rating.

The caveats: leverage is rising, current ratio is below 1, current debt was NZ$336m at FY25, and dividend/growth spending competes with deleveraging. This is serviceable because the assets and cash flows are relatively stable, but it is not a fortress balance sheet.

### Key-Person Risk — 0.76 / confidence 0.70

Genesis is an asset-heavy regulated utility with institutional processes, an independent board, a public-company governance framework and a large operating workforce. The value does not depend on one founder or irreplaceable product visionary. CEO Malcolm Johns matters for Gen35 execution, and the Board approved a restricted share rights offer to retain him through FY28/FY29, but the business would not structurally break if one executive left.

Confidence is capped because I did not do a deep executive-bench/succession review beyond the FY25 integrated report and governance disclosures.

### Incentive Alignment — 0.55 / confidence 0.74

The remuneration framework is mixed. STI is 80% company KPIs and 20% individual KPIs; the FY25 company KPIs include financial performance, customers/people/safety, Huntly Portfolio, renewables, and technology transformation. LTI uses three-year performance share rights tied to absolute and relative TSR, which is shareholder-aligned. FY23 LTI did not vest because TSR and emissions hurdles were not met, which suggests some real pay-for-performance discipline.

Negatives: FY25 STI paid 117% of target despite the shares being a fairly pedestrian utility outcome; the FY25 LTI removed the emissions-reduction hurdle due to external variability and overlap with STI; CEO retention rights are service-based subject to performance/conduct, which may be rational but less performance-sharp. Crown majority ownership (~44% insider/inside holder shown by Yahoo, effectively the NZ Crown mixed-ownership model) also means shareholder alignment is partly political/public-policy shaped rather than purely economic.

### Why the other strategies lost

**Wonderful-and-fair** loses on returns and reinvestment quality: ROIC around 4.5%–6.9% is not a high-return compounding base, and Gen35 is more utility infrastructure execution than a long high-ROIC runway. **Rule-breakers** is not applicable: Genesis is mature, regulated and yield-oriented, not an emerging top-dog disruptor. **Freeroll** has some valuation-floor features — price/book around 0.93 and a 5%+ yield — but lacks a crisp, time-bound re-rating catalyst; the thesis is income plus gradual execution, not a special situation.

## Overall

Genesis is a decent but unspectacular income utility. The positives are clear: essential-service demand, strategic firming assets, covered dividend, positive FCF, a credible yield-plus-growth plan and valuation that is not obviously stretched on book value or FCF. The negatives are equally clear: low returns on capital, meaningful leverage, execution-heavy transition capex, regulatory/political exposure and limited evidence of true pricing power.

The stock belongs on the watchlist as a **cash-cow / income** idea, not a high-conviction compounder. It is attractive enough to monitor, but the margin of safety is not huge while the balance sheet and transition programme still need watching.

## Thesis Connections

Potential FIT angle: energy-transition infrastructure and grid firming. Genesis is not a pure clean-energy growth vehicle because Huntly/Kupe/coal/gas remain central, but it may be a pragmatic transition beneficiary if firming scarcity becomes more valuable while solar, battery and demand-side flexibility scale.
