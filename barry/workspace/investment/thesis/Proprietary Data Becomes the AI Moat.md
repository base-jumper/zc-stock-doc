# Proprietary Data Becomes the AI Moat

## Thesis

As powerful AI models become broadly available, raw intelligence becomes increasingly commoditized. If many companies and individuals can access similar reasoning capability, the durable edge shifts toward the quality, freshness, exclusivity, and usability of the data being fed into those systems.

In this world, proprietary data becomes a more important moat. To make good decisions, AI systems need both intelligence and high-quality inputs. Rubbish in, rubbish out. Companies that own valuable datasets, have legal rights to monetize them, and can package them in AI-friendly formats may become increasingly important infrastructure for decision-making.

The strongest version of this thesis is not simply “data is valuable”. That has been true for decades. The sharper prediction is that **decision-grade proprietary data becomes more valuable as AI increases the speed, scale, and automation of decision-making**.

## Why This Could Happen

- Frontier AI capability is becoming widely accessible through APIs, open-source models, enterprise products, and embedded software.
- As model capability converges, differentiated inputs matter more.
- AI agents need reliable data to make decisions, monitor events, generate research, trigger workflows, and take action.
- Many valuable datasets are not freely available on the open web, are hard to scrape legally, or require specialist cleaning and normalization.
- Enterprises and regulated industries need provenance, citations, auditability, licensing clarity, and compliance — not just scraped text.
- Data owners can potentially charge more if their datasets become machine-consumable fuel for AI workflows rather than static products for human analysts.
- The value of a dataset may increase when it is paired with AI-ready access: APIs, embeddings, structured schemas, real-time feeds, permissioning, metadata, and workflow integrations.

## Key Enablers

- Clear legal ownership or licensing rights over valuable datasets.
- Data that is difficult to replicate, scrape, infer, or synthesize.
- High freshness requirements where real-time or near-real-time updates matter.
- Strong data provenance, citations, audit trails, and compliance controls.
- Agent-friendly access patterns: APIs, structured feeds, query tools, retrieval systems, and LLM-compatible formatting.
- Existing distribution into high-value decision-makers such as investors, banks, insurers, corporates, lawyers, doctors, governments, and real-estate professionals.
- Pricing power from datasets that are embedded into critical workflows.

## Focused Winner Categories

### 1. Financial and Market Data

Financial data is one of the clearest examples of proprietary decision-grade data. Investors already pay heavily for faster, cleaner, broader, and more reliable market information. AI may increase the value of these datasets by making them easier to query, combine, monitor, and act on.

Potential winners include:

- Bloomberg — private, but highly relevant as a benchmark.
- FactSet (`FDS`)
- S&P Global (`SPGI`)
- MSCI (`MSCI`)
- Moody's (`MCO`)
- Intercontinental Exchange (`ICE`)
- London Stock Exchange Group (`LSEG.L`)
- Morningstar (`MORN`)
- Nasdaq (`NDAQ`)
- CME Group (`CME`)
- Cboe (`CBOE`)

What to investigate:

- Which providers have unique or hard-to-replicate datasets?
- Which have pricing power and high customer retention?
- Which are embedding AI into workflows rather than just adding chatbots?
- Which can sell data directly into AI agents, research systems, and automated monitoring tools?

### 2. Alternative Data

Alternative data providers collect non-traditional datasets that can improve decisions in finance, real estate, logistics, retail, insurance, and macro analysis. These can include web traffic, app usage, credit card transactions, geolocation, satellite imagery, shipping flows, job postings, pricing data, and other behavioral or operational signals.

Potential winner types include:

- Public companies with unique transaction or behavioral datasets.
- Data brokers with legally licensed proprietary datasets.
- Satellite and geospatial data providers.
- Web, app, pricing, employment, and consumer-behavior data providers.
- Companies that turn messy alternative data into decision-ready signals.

Specific names to investigate:

- Similarweb (`SMWB`) — web and app traffic intelligence.
- CoStar (`CSGP`) — real estate data; also fits the real-estate category below.
- Verisk (`VRSK`) — insurance, risk, catastrophe, and industry datasets.
- TransUnion (`TRU`) / Equifax (`EFX`) / Experian (`EXPN.L`) — credit and consumer data, though regulatory/privacy risk is significant.
- FICO (`FICO`) — scoring and analytics layer attached to credit data.
- Planet Labs (`PL`) — satellite imagery and geospatial data, higher risk/speculative.

What to investigate:

- Which datasets are genuinely proprietary versus easily scraped or replicated?
- Which providers have permissioned access and clean legal rights?
- Which datasets are valuable enough to support premium pricing?
- Which customers use the data for high-stakes decisions?

### 3. Real Estate Data

Real estate data is worth treating as a major category because it resembles market data: property values, transactions, listings, ownership, rents, vacancy, foot traffic, tenant quality, mortgage risk, development pipelines, and local supply/demand conditions can all influence high-value decisions.

Potential winners include:

- CoStar (`CSGP`) — commercial real estate data and marketplaces.
- Zillow (`Z`) / (`ZG`) — residential property data and consumer traffic.
- REA Group (`REA.AX`) — Australian property listings and audience data.
- Rightmove (`RMV.L`) — UK property listings and property-search data.
- Domain (`DHG.AX`) — Australian property portal data.
- CoreLogic — private, but relevant as a benchmark for property, mortgage, and insurance datasets.

What to investigate:

- Which companies own the deepest property transaction, listing, rent, and ownership datasets?
- Which data is exclusive versus syndicated or publicly available?
- Which platforms control both data and customer workflow?
- Which have enough market coverage to become default AI data sources for property decisions?

### 4. Vertical Proprietary Databases

Some of the best data moats may exist in vertical markets where the data is specialized, regulated, fragmented, or deeply embedded into workflows.

Potential verticals:

- Healthcare and life sciences: clinical, drug, claims, lab, genomic, provider, and patient journey data.
- Legal and regulatory: case law, filings, contracts, sanctions, compliance, and legislative data.
- Insurance and risk: underwriting, claims, catastrophe, fraud, and asset-risk datasets.
- Supply chain and logistics: shipping, trade, inventory, supplier, customs, and operational data.
- Scientific and technical research: journals, patents, citations, experiments, and specialist databases.

Specific names to investigate:

- RELX (`REL.L`) — LexisNexis, legal, risk, scientific and technical information.
- Thomson Reuters (`TRI`) — legal, tax, accounting, regulatory, and professional data.
- Wolters Kluwer (`WKL.AS`) — legal, tax, accounting, compliance, healthcare information.
- Verisk (`VRSK`) — insurance and risk data.
- IQVIA (`IQV`) — healthcare, clinical, and pharmaceutical datasets.
- Clarivate (`CLVT`) — research, intellectual property, and patent data; quality and leverage need investigation.
- Informa (`INF.L`) — specialist information and events data; may be less pure.

What to investigate:

- Which verticals have data that is both valuable and legally defensible?
- Which providers are already embedded into professional workflows?
- Which datasets improve materially when exposed through AI search, summarization, monitoring, or agentic workflows?
- Which companies have strong margins and recurring revenue from data access?

### 5. AI-Ready Data Layers

This is the most directly AI-native category. These companies do not merely own data; they package it so AI systems can use it effectively. Bigdata.com is a good example: it aggregates and structures financial information into a platform designed for AI agents, grounded search, verifiable insights, and automated research workflows.

Potential winners include:

- Existing data companies that successfully convert their datasets into AI-native products.
- Startups that aggregate licensed data and expose it in LLM-friendly formats.
- Platforms that provide cited, permissioned, real-time data access to enterprise AI agents.
- Companies that sit between proprietary data owners and AI application builders.

Specific examples / benchmarks:

- Bigdata.com / RavenPack — private, finance-focused, agentic research and monitoring platform.
- Bloomberg — if it becomes a default AI data/workflow layer for finance professionals.
- FactSet, S&P Global, LSEG, MSCI, Morningstar — if they successfully expose proprietary data through AI-native workflows.
- Vertical incumbents like RELX, Thomson Reuters, Wolters Kluwer, IQVIA, and Verisk — if they turn their data products into AI-agent substrates.

What to investigate:

- Are customers paying for AI-ready data access, or is it bundled as a defensive feature?
- Does AI increase pricing power, usage, or retention?
- Are these products truly grounded in proprietary data, or just generic AI wrappers?
- Can independent AI-ready data aggregators get defensible access to enough high-value licensed data?

## What Is Out Of Scope

- General data infrastructure companies such as Snowflake, Databricks, MongoDB, and cloud data warehouses. These are data-adjacent but typically cannot sell customer data and may not own differentiated datasets themselves.
- Identity, trust, authentication, and verification data. This is real and potentially valuable, but belongs in a separate FIT.
- Generic AI model providers unless they own or control unique proprietary datasets.
- Companies whose only edge is scraping public data without durable rights, exclusivity, or workflow integration.

## What To Monitor

- Data providers launching AI-native products with clear monetization, not just marketing demos.
- Earnings-call mentions of AI increasing data usage, customer retention, upsell, or pricing power.
- Partnerships between AI platforms and proprietary data owners.
- Enterprise AI agents requiring licensed data access for finance, legal, healthcare, real estate, insurance, or compliance workflows.
- Legal cases or regulation that strengthen data owners' ability to charge AI companies for access.
- Data providers exposing APIs, retrieval systems, citations, provenance, and audit trails for AI use cases.
- Evidence that customers are moving from seat-based human data products to usage-based machine/agent data access.
- New AI-native data aggregators gaining traction, especially in finance and professional services.
- Margin expansion or revenue acceleration at data providers attributable to AI-driven demand.

## Risks / Bear Case

- Data access becomes commoditized because AI systems can infer, scrape, or synthesize enough information from public sources.
- Model providers capture most of the economics by bundling data access into AI platforms.
- Data owners lack the technical execution to build useful AI-native products.
- Customers resist paying more for data if AI reduces the need for human analyst seats.
- Legal or regulatory changes restrict the collection, sale, or reuse of proprietary datasets.
- Privacy backlash damages consumer, credit, location, and behavioral data businesses.
- Open data initiatives reduce the scarcity value of certain datasets.
- AI hallucination or compliance failures make enterprises cautious about automating decisions even with good data.

## Initial Probability

High conviction on the broad theme: proprietary, decision-grade data should become more important as AI reasoning becomes cheaper and more widely available.

Medium conviction on specific winners: the best companies may be existing data monopolies with pricing power, but the market may also create new AI-native data intermediaries.

Initial probability estimate: ~75% that proprietary data access becomes a more important source of competitive advantage over the next 5–10 years; ~55–60% that this creates clearly identifiable public-market winners with material AI-driven revenue upside.
