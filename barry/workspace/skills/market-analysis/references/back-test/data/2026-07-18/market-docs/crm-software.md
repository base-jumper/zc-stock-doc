# CRM Software

## Market Definition

This market covers packaged customer relationship management application software sold by license, maintenance, hosted subscription, or on-demand subscription. Included functions are sales force automation, customer service and support, marketing automation, contact-center applications, customer analytics, and CRM-specific customization or integration platforms bundled with the application.

It excludes consulting and systems-integration labor, internally built custom CRM, generic database and middleware revenue, telecom contact-center infrastructure, ERP modules not sold as CRM, and broader customer-service business-process outsourcing. The TAM is vendor application revenue, not customer implementation spend.

Maturity means CRM has become a standard enterprise and midmarket application category, on-demand delivery is accepted for a material share of deployments, and replacement/expansion demand is driven more by installed-base renewal, adjacent modules, and suite consolidation than first-time experimentation.

## Current View

The base-year market is recovering from the post-2000 enterprise software recession. Salesforce's IPO prospectus cites IDC's estimate that CRM applications were about `$7.1B` in 2002, while Gartner reported that new CRM license revenue fell to `$2.8B` in 2002 from `$3.7B` in 2001. Because the broader application revenue base includes maintenance and hosted subscriptions rather than only new license sales, the stored 2004 current TAM estimate is `$8.2B`.

Hosted and on-demand CRM is still a small but credible segment. Salesforce had grown from its February 2000 launch to 9,800 customers and 147,000 paying subscriptions by April 30, 2004, then reported about 12,500 customers and 195,000 paying subscriptions by October 31, 2004. Its nine-month revenue through October 31, 2004 was `$121.8M`, with 89% from subscription and support. That is still less than 2% of the estimated total CRM applications market, but the growth rate and subscription gross margin show that delivery model risk is no longer theoretical.

The market remains difficult: Salesforce itself warns that enterprises may resist third-party hosting of critical customer data, larger customers may require customization, and established vendors have much greater resources. Siebel's Q3 2004 license revenue was still only `$104.6M`, down 5% year over year, despite sequential recovery. The live 2004 view is therefore a market-transition thesis, not a clean hypergrowth software category.

## Adoption Path

Adoption should proceed through three overlapping waves. First, large enterprises continue buying CRM suites from Siebel, SAP, Oracle/PeopleSoft, and vertical specialists, but demand shifts toward lower-risk upgrades, analytics, and industry templates after many expensive implementations underperformed. Second, midmarket buyers adopt simpler hosted or Microsoft-linked systems because they lack large implementation budgets. Third, successful on-demand vendors move upmarket as security, integration, reliability, and customization improve.

Salesforce's model directly attacks the historic CRM pain points: high upfront license cost, long implementation cycles, low user adoption, and poor access outside the office. IDC's forecast in Salesforce's prospectus put on-demand application services growing from `$425M` in 2002 to `$2.6B` in 2007. If CRM is one of the first categories to convert, subscription delivery can expand the served market by reaching smaller firms and departmental deployments that would not have bought a full Siebel-style implementation.

The limiting factors are enterprise trust, integration with ERP and legacy data, channel reach, and feature depth. On-premise suites remain advantaged for complex global accounts, regulated industries, and buyers that want one strategic enterprise applications vendor.

## Market Structure

The mature HHI estimate of `0.13` implies an oligopoly with meaningful long-tail participation. CRM has scale advantages from brand, implementation partners, installed-base maintenance, integration APIs, and sales distribution, but it lacks the pure network effects that would justify a winner-take-most forecast. Gartner's 2002 new-license shares already show leadership without monopoly: Siebel 24.9%, SAP 15.9%, PeopleSoft 4.3%, Oracle 4.3%, and Amdocs 3.2%.

Consolidation should raise concentration. Oracle and PeopleSoft signed a definitive merger agreement in December 2004, and Siebel is actively responding to competition with hosted CRM and vertical applications. At the same time, the remaining market is fragmented across Amdocs, Sage, RightNow, NetSuite, Salesnet, E.piphany, Onyx, Pivotal, BMC, FrontRange, vertical products, and internal systems.

The main structural question is whether delivery architecture becomes the control point. If multi-tenant on-demand software proves secure and configurable for complex enterprises, Salesforce can take disproportionate share. If customers mainly value CRM as an extension of ERP, SAP and Oracle should dominate.

## Players

Siebel Systems is the incumbent leader and broadest pure-play CRM vendor. It has deep enterprise functionality, a large customer base, partner familiarity, analytics, vertical initiatives, and its own CRM OnDemand response. The risk is that its license model and implementation complexity are exactly what customers want to reduce.

SAP is the strongest ERP-suite challenger. Its CRM share rose sharply in Gartner's 2002 data, and its advantage is account control in enterprises already standardized on SAP. The risk is that CRM bought as part of an ERP footprint may be slower to deploy and less attractive to front-office users than a specialist product.

Oracle / PeopleSoft should become a larger applications competitor once the PeopleSoft deal closes. Oracle brings database, middleware, financial resources, and acquisition appetite; PeopleSoft adds applications customers and JD Edwards midmarket exposure. Integration distraction and customer uncertainty are the main risks.

Salesforce.com is the highest-upside model challenger. It has the clearest on-demand brand, rapid customer/subscriber growth, and a subscription revenue model aligned with lower upfront risk. Its mature capture is capped in this forecast because in 2004 it is still young, small, dependent on direct sales, and unproven in the most complex enterprise deployments.

Microsoft is an early but credible midmarket entrant. Microsoft CRM 1.2 became globally available in January 2004, targets companies with 25 to 1,000 employees, integrates with Outlook and Windows Server, and can use Microsoft's reseller channel. Its limitation is product depth versus dedicated CRM vendors.

## Watch

Track Salesforce renewal rates, enterprise customer wins, uptime/security incidents, deferred revenue growth, and API/platform adoption. Track whether Siebel can make hosted CRM material without damaging license economics. Watch SAP and Oracle attach rates inside ERP accounts, Microsoft CRM reseller traction, and whether customers standardize on one CRM suite or keep using point solutions by function and vertical.

Revise the market view if on-demand vendors prove they can support complex enterprise customization faster than expected, if security failures slow hosted adoption, if Oracle continues consolidating major CRM assets, or if Microsoft turns CRM into an Office/Outlook distribution play.
