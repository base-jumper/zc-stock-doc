# Growing Wealth Divide

## Thesis

Economic growth driven by the AI boom may make the rich richer. The world could become wealthier overall, but with greater concentration of wealth in the hands of capital owners, top executives, elite technical talent, and people who already own productive assets.

AI can increase corporate margins by reducing reliance on human labour, automating white-collar work, improving decision-making, and increasing output per employee. If most of those gains accrue to shareholders rather than labour, stock prices and asset values may continue to rise while many workers face stagnant wages, displacement, or a tougher employment market.

The thesis is not simply “inequality rises”. The investable expression is that more wealth flows into financial assets, alternative assets, luxury/status goods, private markets, data centres, infrastructure, and specialised investment managers that serve wealthy individuals and institutions.

## Initial Probability

~70% over a 5–7 year horizon.

This feels highly plausible, but the path is politically sensitive. If inequality rises too quickly or visibly, policy responses such as higher taxes, regulation, UBI, labour protections, anti-monopoly enforcement, or AI-specific rules could change the investment expression.

## Why This Could Happen

- AI increases returns to capital, compute, data, distribution, brands, and proprietary workflows.
- Companies may produce more output with fewer employees.
- Labour share of income may fall if productivity gains accrue mainly to shareholders.
- Wealthy households own more financial assets and therefore benefit disproportionately from rising markets.
- AI leaders may develop winner-take-most dynamics due to scale, data, compute, and distribution advantages.
- High-income individuals may use AI to become even more productive, while lower-skilled labour has less leverage.
- Displaced workers may move into lower-paid real-world service work that AI/robots cannot yet perform.

## Human Labour Angle

As AI takes over more cognitive and administrative work, some remaining jobs may shift toward physical-world tasks that AI cannot perform until robotics catches up. Examples might include:

- Walking supermarket aisles to pick grocery orders.
- Collecting food orders from within malls and handing them to autonomous delivery vehicles.
- Local errands, inspections, cleaning, maintenance, setup, and verification tasks.
- Care work, hospitality, security, and other human-presence roles.

This overlaps with the separate FIT: **AI Tasking Human Freelancers**.

## Broad Winners

- Alternative asset managers and hedge fund operators serving wealthy clients and institutions.
- Private credit, private equity, infrastructure, real assets, and resource royalty platforms.
- Data centre, energy, grid, and infrastructure owners/developers.
- Luxury and status-goods companies.
- Wealth management, private banking, custody, and brokerage platforms.
- Exchanges, index providers, financial data vendors, and market infrastructure companies.
- High-quality asset managers with differentiated access, relationships, or opportunity origination.
- Security/defence/public-safety providers if inequality contributes to social instability.

## Fund Manager Selection Criteria

Stock-picking itself may be vulnerable to AI automation. Why pay high fees for public-market stock selection if an agent can analyse public information cheaply? Therefore, the strongest asset-manager winners should have advantages that are difficult for AI alone to replicate.

More AI-proof traits include:

- Exposure to non-exchange-traded assets such as private equity, natural resources, private credit, infrastructure, water, property, and royalties.
- Opportunity initiation: seeding companies, building assets, structuring deals, developing data centres, infrastructure, or energy projects.
- Proprietary relationships with founders, management teams, governments, capital partners, and asset owners.
- Access to scarce deals that are not available in public markets.
- Strong track records that attract institutional and high-net-worth flows.
- Permanent or sticky capital.
- Ability to earn performance fees, not only management fees.

It can benefit from rising funds under management and performance fees if wealthy/institutional capital continues moving into differentiated strategies.

### HMC Capital (`HMC`)

HMC invests across data centres, green energy, healthcare real estate, retail real estate, and other real assets. It scores highly on opportunity initiation, especially if it is actively building or structuring data centre exposure rather than merely managing passive assets.

As an HMC shareholder, the upside is not only management fees. HMC also owns meaningful stakes in some underlying funds/assets, creating balance-sheet exposure alongside asset-management economics.

Things to watch:

- Ability to raise new funds in attractive verticals.

### Brookfield / Blackstone / KKR / Apollo

Large global alternative asset managers with exposure to private equity, infrastructure, private credit, real estate, energy transition, and institutional capital flows. They may be broad winners if wealth concentration and institutional allocation to alternatives continue.

Things to watch:
- Fee-related earnings.
- Permanent capital growth.
- Private credit growth.
- Infrastructure/data centre exposure.
- Fundraising environment.
- Realisation activity and performance fees.

### Luxury / Status Goods

If wealth concentration rises, high-end consumption may remain resilient or grow. Potential beneficiaries include LVMH, Hermès, Ferrari, Richemont, and other luxury/status brands.

Things to watch:
- High-end consumer demand.
- Geographic mix, especially US/China.
- Pricing power.
- Margin resilience during economic slowdowns.

### Market Infrastructure

Exchanges, index providers, ratings agencies, and financial data companies may benefit from rising asset values, trading activity, passive flows, and more complex financial markets.

Potential names: CME, ICE, Nasdaq, MSCI, S&P Global, Moody’s, LSEG.

Things to watch:
- Market data revenue.
- Index/licensing revenue.
- Trading volumes.
- AUM linked to indices.
- Demand for private market data and analytics.

## Risks / Bear Case

- AI productivity gains are broadly distributed through lower prices, higher wages, or new job creation.
- Governments redistribute gains through taxes, transfers, UBI, or public ownership/sovereign AI infrastructure.
- AI disrupts high-income white-collar roles more than low-income roles, reducing inequality rather than increasing it.
- Asset managers are disrupted by AI-driven low-cost investment agents.
- Alternative asset valuations become stretched and future returns disappoint.
- Populist backlash imposes regulation, fee pressure, or higher taxation on perceived winners.
- High interest rates or credit losses hurt private markets and asset managers.

## Investment Framing

This thesis is partly macro and partly sector selection. The investable version should focus less on making a broad moral claim about inequality and more on identifying where incremental wealth and capital flows go.

The best winners are likely to be businesses with:

- Exposure to wealthy clients or institutional capital.
- Scarce assets or proprietary deal access.
- Pricing power.
- High incremental margins.
- Long-duration fee streams.
- Real asset/data centre/infrastructure exposure.
- Some protection from AI commoditising public-market analysis.

The main thing to watch is whether AI productivity is accruing to labour, consumers, governments, or capital. If capital captures most of it, this thesis strengthens. If gains are broadly distributed or heavily taxed, conviction should fall.
