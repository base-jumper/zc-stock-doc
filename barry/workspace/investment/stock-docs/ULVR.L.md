# ULVR.L — Unilever PLC

## Business Overview

Unilever is a global consumer-staples company selling branded beauty, personal care, home care and food products across developed and emerging markets. After the 2025 Ice Cream demerger, the group is simpler and more weighted toward higher-margin, faster-growing categories: Beauty & Wellbeing (€12.8b 2025 turnover), Personal Care (€13.2b), Home Care (€11.6b), and Foods (€12.9b).

FY2025 turnover was **€50.5b**, with **3.5% underlying sales growth** and **1.5% underlying volume growth**. Power Brands were **78% of turnover** and grew faster at **4.3% USG / 2.2% UVG**. Profitability is strong for staples: **46.9% gross margin**, **20.0% underlying operating margin**, **€3.08 underlying EPS**, and **€5.9b free cash flow**. Q1 2026 was better quality than 2025: **3.8% USG**, driven by **2.9% volume** and only **0.9% price**, with Power Brands at **5.0% USG / 4.0% volume**.

The current shape is not hyper-growth; it is a mature branded cash machine trying to improve mix, premiumisation and execution. The Ice Cream demerger and announced Foods combination with McCormick push the company toward a cleaner Beauty/Personal Care/Home Care profile, but the investment case still rests mainly on durable cash generation and disciplined capital returns.

## Trait Assessment

### Earnings Quality / Consistent, Predictable Profits — 0.82 / confidence 0.85

Unilever has demonstrated consistent earning power. FY2025 operating profit was **€9.0b**, underlying operating profit **€10.1b**, diluted EPS **€2.59**, and underlying EPS **€3.08**, up slightly from re-presented 2024 underlying EPS of **€3.06** and 2023 **€2.70**. The business is not immune to currency, cost inflation or local-market disruption, but consumer staples demand and brand portfolios make the earnings stream more predictable than most operating companies.

Cash backs the earnings: FY2025 free cash flow was **€5.9b** versus underlying profit attributable to shareholders of **€6.8b**, and management reports **100% cash conversion**. Reported FCF fell from €6.3b in 2024 because of Ice Cream demerger tax/cost effects, not because the core stopped converting profit to cash.

Accounting quality is decent but not pristine: Unilever uses underlying/non-underlying adjustments and restructuring costs are a recurring feature of a portfolio company. That keeps the score below excellent, but the long record of profitability and cash conversion is strong.

### Free Cash Flow Generation — 0.78 / confidence 0.85

FY2025 FCF was **€5.9b** on **€50.5b** turnover, an FCF margin of roughly **11.7%**. That is not software-like, but it is very strong for branded consumer staples, especially with ongoing brand investment at **16.1% of turnover**. Net capital expenditure was **€1.465b** against operating cash flow of **€10.772b** before tax/interest adjustments, so capex intensity is low; Unilever’s cash drain is tax, interest and shareholder returns, not a need to constantly rebuild the asset base.

The cash looks genuinely distributable: the company returned **€6.0b** to shareholders in 2025, comprising dividends and **€1.5b** of buybacks, close to the year’s FCF. The main caveat is that FCF can be hit by restructuring, demerger costs, working-capital swings and acquisitions; normalised cash generation is still strong, but not perfectly clean.

### Fundamental Stability / Secular Durability — 0.78 / confidence 0.80

The core demand base is durable: hygiene, beauty, deodorants, skin cleansing, laundry, dressings and cooking aids are recurring, global categories. Q1 2026 growth was volume-led, which matters because the 2022–2024 staples cycle often leaned heavily on price. Emerging markets grew **5.7%** in Q1 2026 with **4.2% volume**, led by India, giving a credible persistence story.

The threats are real rather than existential: private label, local insurgent brands, retailer bargaining power, commodity/currency pressure, and slower developed-market category growth. Unilever’s response — Power Brands, premiumisation, digital commerce, sharper portfolio focus — is plausible and already visible in Power Brand outperformance. I do not see a melting-ice-cube pattern; I see a mature franchise that needs execution to sustain mid-single-digit growth.

Confidence would firm up with the FY2026 annual report showing whether Q1 volume momentum persisted across the full year after the Foods transaction announcement.

### Pricing Power / Durable Moat — 0.76 / confidence 0.80

Unilever’s moat is mostly brand, distribution scale, emerging-market route-to-market, category knowledge and marketing muscle. The evidence is visible in the numbers: **46.9% gross margin**, **20.0% underlying operating margin**, and Power Brands outperforming the group. The company has also invested heavily behind brands, with brand and marketing investment at **16.1% of turnover**, up 300bps over five years.

Pricing power is real, but not absolute. Q1 2026 growth was mostly volume rather than price, which is healthy for demand but shows the post-inflation pricing tailwind has faded. In many categories Unilever faces intense competition from P&G, L’Oréal, Colgate, Reckitt, Nestlé, private label and local champions. The moat is wide enough to sustain good margins, but not so wide that the company can raise price indefinitely without volume consequences.

### Capital Allocation — 0.72 / confidence 0.75

Capital allocation has improved. The Ice Cream demerger created a simpler company, management is explicitly prioritising Beauty, Wellbeing and Personal Care, and the retained **19.85%** TMICC stake is intended to be sold down to cover demerger costs and preserve flexibility. Management also states a capital allocation framework of roughly **60% dividend payout**, surplus-cash buybacks, bolt-on M&A and no transformational M&A.

The 2025 actions fit that framework: **€6.0b** returned to shareholders, including **€1.5b** buybacks; targeted bolt-ons such as Dr. Squatch, Minimalist and Wild; and disposals of non-core/local brands. The caveat is Unilever’s historical record includes sprawling portfolio complexity and expensive brand management bureaucracy. The current plan is sensible, but still needs a few years of evidence that bolt-ons and portfolio moves actually improve per-share value.

Confidence would improve after reviewing the full business-combinations notes and subsequent performance of the 2025 bolt-ons.

### High, Durable Returns on Capital — 0.78 / confidence 0.85

Underlying ROIC was **19.0%** in 2025, essentially stable versus **19.1%** in 2024, and management targets high-teens ROIC. That is a strong return for a mature branded consumer goods company and supports the view that the brand/distribution moat is economically real.

This is not a capital-light pure compounder with 30%+ reinvestment returns everywhere. The group owns manufacturing, distribution, inventories and a large goodwill/intangibles base. But the reported ROIC is high enough above likely cost of capital to create value, and its stability through inflation/currency pressure is a good sign. Incremental returns should improve if portfolio simplification works; they could fade if premiumisation disappoints or acquisitions dilute the base.

### Conservative Debt / Financial Strength — 0.68 / confidence 0.80

The balance sheet is acceptable but not fortress-like. Closing net debt was **€23.1b** at FY2025, down from €24.5b, and net debt / underlying EBITDA was **2.0x**. For a stable staples business this is manageable, and the group targets roughly 2x net debt/EBITDA with strong single-A credit ratings.

The reason this does not score higher is that leverage is meaningful, interest/tax cash outflows are material, and the company is also paying a large dividend plus buybacks. Debt is matched to stable cash flows, not a solvency concern, but it would constrain Unilever if growth falters or if management overdoes M&A/shareholder returns.

### Key-Person Risk — 0.86 / confidence 0.80

Low key-person risk. Unilever’s advantage sits in brands, distribution, marketing systems, supplier relationships and global scale — not in one irreplaceable founder or CEO. Fernando Fernandez may matter for execution and portfolio discipline, but the business would not break if one executive left.

The more relevant risk is organisational: a big consumer-goods group can drift into bureaucracy, slow decision-making and diluted brand focus. That is not key-person fragility; it is operating-culture risk, and the recent simplification plan is meant to address it.

### Incentive Alignment — 0.72 / confidence 0.75

The incentive system is mostly pointed the right way. The 2026 annual bonus uses **40% underlying sales growth**, **30% underlying operating profit growth less restructuring**, and **30% free cash flow**. The 2026 PSP uses **25% USG**, **30% relative TSR**, **30% average underlying ROIC**, and **15% Sustainability Progress Index**. ROIC and FCF are exactly the right guardrails for a mature branded cash generator; relative TSR adds shareholder alignment.

Weak spots: annual bonus still rewards near-term growth/profit optics, and the SPI component may or may not map tightly to per-share value depending on execution. Insider founder-style ownership is absent because this is an old institutional company. Overall, though, the plan has enough ROIC/FCF/TSR weight to avoid rewarding pure empire-building.

### Why the other strategies lost

**Wonderful and Fair** is plausible but secondary. Unilever scores well on earnings quality, ROIC, pricing power and balance-sheet adequacy, but the reinvestment runway is only moderate. This is a mature staples franchise returning surplus cash, not a business that can redeploy most earnings at high returns for a decade.

**Rule Breakers** fails structurally: Unilever is not an emerging category-defining disruptor with accelerating growth. **Freeroll** also fails: there is no hard valuation floor plus time-bound catalyst; portfolio simplification helps, but the setup is not an asymmetric re-rating trade.

## Overall

Unilever is a good **cash-cow** candidate: durable brands, strong margins, high-teens ROIC, real FCF, a covered dividend, and a clear capital-return framework. It is not exciting, but it is high-quality enough that boredom is part of the appeal.

The detractors are valuation and maturity. At roughly **17.3x** current underlying EPS, the stock is not obviously cheap; expected returns come from mid-single-digit earnings growth, dividends and modest buybacks, not a big rerating. Execution risk also matters: the company has to prove that the Ice Cream/Foods reshaping and premiumisation agenda lift growth without diluting returns through M&A or restructuring churn.
