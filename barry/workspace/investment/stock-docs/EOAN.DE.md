# EOAN.DE — E.ON SE

## Business Overview

E.ON is a Germany-based European utility focused on regulated/contracted energy infrastructure and customer supply rather than merchant generation. Its core profit engine is **Energy Networks**: electricity and gas distribution grids across Germany and other European markets. It also runs **Energy Infrastructure Solutions** and **Energy Retail**, serving customers with power, gas and energy-transition services. The business is capital-intensive and regulated: returns depend on allowed network returns, regulatory asset-base growth, financing costs, execution of grid capex, and retail margin discipline.

The current case is a steady utility compounder built around electrification and grid reinforcement. E.ON reported FY2025 **adjusted Group EBITDA of €9.8b** and **adjusted Group net income of €3.0b**, and lifted its 2026–2030 investment plan to **€48b** with a 2030 target of about **€13b adjusted EBITDA** and **€3.8b adjusted net income**. Q1 2026 was on track: adjusted EBITDA rose about **2% to €3.3b**, adjusted net income about **7% to €1.3b**, and full-year 2026 guidance was reaffirmed.

## Trait Assessment

### Earnings Quality / Consistent, Predictable Profits — 0.55 / confidence 0.75

The underlying utility earnings base is real, but statutory earnings are not especially clean. Yahoo shows positive net income in each available year from 2022–2025, but the path is lumpy: diluted EPS was **€0.70, €0.20, €1.73, €0.66**, and cumulative reported FCF was only about **0.33×** cumulative net income. Management’s adjusted earnings are smoother — FY2025 adjusted net income was **€3.0b**, with 2026 guidance of **€2.7–2.9b** adjusted net income — but that also means the thesis leans on adjusted rather than statutory figures.

The score is mid-band: E.ON has a durable regulated earnings base and no recent loss years in the Yahoo history, but cash conversion is weak during the current investment cycle and reported results include unusual/regulatory effects. Confidence would improve with a deeper 7–10 year adjusted EPS and regulatory-adjustment bridge.

### High, Durable Returns on Capital — 0.62 / confidence 0.75

Returns look decent for a regulated utility, not exceptional on an absolute basis. Yahoo-derived ROIC is high because invested-capital accounting is distorted by goodwill and utility balance-sheet structure, but ROCE is a more conservative cross-check: **6.3% in 2025**, **11.6% in 2024**, **3.3% in 2023**, and **4.5% in 2022**. The spread over utility funding costs appears positive in normal years, and incremental investment should earn regulated returns, but the returns are not the kind of high-margin, capital-light economics that would score near the top of the rubric.

This is enough to support a regulated compounder thesis, especially if the €48b capex plan earns allowed returns, but the capital-heavy model and goodwill-heavy balance sheet cap the score.

### Pricing Power / Durable Moat — 0.70 / confidence 0.75

The moat is structural rather than brand-led. Distribution grids are local monopoly infrastructure with regulated returns and high barriers to duplication. That gives E.ON a durable position and a mechanism to recover prudent investment over time. Retail energy supply is more competitive and lower-moat, but the group’s center of gravity is networks.

The live risk is regulation: pricing power is mediated by regulators, not freely exercised. Allowed returns, tariff timing and political affordability pressure can delay or reduce recovery. I score the moat high enough for a utility network owner, but not as a fully discretionary pricing-power business.

### Reinvestment Runway — 0.78 / confidence 0.80

This is E.ON’s strongest Wonderful-and-Fair trait. European electrification, grid modernisation, distributed renewables, heat pumps, EVs and data-center/grid load all require distribution-network investment. E.ON’s own plan calls for **€48b of investment across 2026–2030**, up from the prior €43b 2024–2028 plan, and management targets adjusted EBITDA growth from **€9.8b in 2025** to about **€13b by 2030**.

The runway is large and visible, though not risk-free: it depends on adequate regulatory frameworks, permitting/execution, and earning acceptable returns on the expanded asset base. This is a long regulated reinvestment runway, not a high-ROIC software-style runway.

### Capital Allocation — 0.58 / confidence 0.70

Management is directing most capital into regulated networks, which is rational if allowed returns stay adequate. Total FY2025 investment was reported at **€8.5b**, including roughly **€7.0b in Energy Networks**, matching the thesis. Dividends have also risen steadily, from **€0.43/share in 2019** to **€0.57/share in 2026**.

The caveat is that free cash flow is negative during the investment cycle. Yahoo shows capex exceeding operating cash flow in 2023–2025 and dividends paid despite negative reported FCF. That can be fine for a regulated utility building its asset base, but it means the dividend is effectively supported by earnings/regulatory value creation and balance-sheet capacity rather than current free cash. Score: sensible strategic allocation, but not a clean self-funded cash compounding machine.

### Conservative Debt / Financial Strength — 0.62 / confidence 0.75

Leverage is acceptable for regulated infrastructure but worth monitoring. Yahoo’s current snapshot shows total debt about **€43.2b**, cash about **€5.4b**, net debt about **€37.8b**, and net debt/EBITDA about **3.9×**. That is within the broad conservative-to-stretched range for a regulated utility. Interest cover is more mixed: TTM EBIT interest cover is about **4.5×**, while annual EBIT cover was **3.1×** in 2025, **5.6×** in 2024, and weaker during 2023’s noisy year.

This is not an imminent solvency concern, so no disqualifier fires. But rising rates, regulatory lag and heavy capex mean debt can constrain equity returns if allowed returns do not keep up.

### Key-Person Risk — 0.85 / confidence 0.75

The investment case is institutional: regulated assets, licences, operating systems, financing access and regulatory relationships. It does not depend on one founder or star allocator. A CEO change would matter for execution and regulatory tone, but it would not break the network-utility thesis.

### Incentive Alignment — 0.65 / confidence 0.55

Yahoo’s governance snapshot is favourable, with low headline risk scores, and the business model naturally pushes management toward long-term regulatory asset growth and reliability. That is directionally aligned with owners if investment earns allowed returns.

Confidence is only moderate because I did not inspect the latest remuneration report in detail. The key follow-up is whether incentives are tied to per-share value, ROCE/ROIC, reliability/customer outcomes and decarbonisation execution rather than simply size, EBITDA or capex growth.

**Why the other strategies lost:** Cash Cow loses despite the dividend history because reported FCF is negative and capex currently exceeds OCF; this is an investment-phase regulated utility, not surplus-cash harvesting. Rule Breakers loses because E.ON is mature infrastructure, not an emerging category dominator. Freeroll loses because there is no hard floor or concrete time-bound catalyst; the thesis is steady regulated compounding, not a re-rate event.

## Overall

E.ON looks like a reasonable **Wonderful-and-Fair / regulated reinvestment** candidate, but not a screaming bargain. The assets are durable, the reinvestment runway is unusually visible, and management has a credible 2030 earnings target. The main detractors are capital intensity, regulatory dependence, only moderate free-cash conversion, and a valuation that already prices in a fair amount of stability.

At **€17.66**, the stock trades around **16×** my current adjusted EPS anchor and about **14×** Yahoo’s next-year consensus EPS. That is not excessive for a resilient grid-heavy utility, but the expected return relies on allowed earnings growth and dividends, not multiple expansion. I would keep it watched, with most attention on whether network capex converts into regulated earnings without leverage creeping too far.
