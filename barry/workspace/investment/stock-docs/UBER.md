# UBER — Uber Technologies, Inc.

## Business Overview

Uber is a global mobility, delivery and freight marketplace. Mobility connects riders with drivers across rideshare, taxi, rentals and related transport products; Delivery connects consumers with restaurants, grocery, retail and white-label delivery; Freight is a smaller logistics brokerage/managed-transport business. The core profit engine is marketplace scale: more riders/eaters, drivers/couriers and merchants improve liquidity, ETAs, utilization and ad/membership monetisation.

The latest operating evidence is strong. In Q2 2026 MAPCs rose 16% to 208m, Trips rose 18% to 3.87b, Gross Bookings rose 24% to $58.0b (22% constant currency), and revenue rose 12% despite business-model changes cutting reported growth by eight points. Operating income rose 30% to $1.9b, adjusted EBITDA rose 33% to $2.8b, and FCF rose 13% to $2.8b, taking trailing-12-month FCF above $10b. GAAP net income of $2.4b included a $1.6b pre-tax investment revaluation benefit, so operating income and FCF remain the cleaner measures.

The main segments are Mobility and Delivery. Mobility remains the higher-margin cash engine; Delivery is still compounding bookings and adding advertising. Freight is much less central and more cyclical. Uber One, advertising, grocery/retail, airport/enterprise use cases, autonomous-vehicle partnerships and international penetration are the incremental reinvestment runway.

Capital allocation is now a central part of the thesis. Uber increased its Delivery Hero stake to 24.99% for $2.3b in Q2, added $1.6b of Delivery Hero-linked total-return swaps, and on July 16 agreed to offer €41.50 per share for the rest of Delivery Hero, implying a $14.8b equity value for 100%. The offer is expected to close in the second half of 2027 and will be funded with cash and new debt. On August 6 Uber replaced €4.0b of its €14.2b bridge commitment with unsecured, rating-priced term loans maturing 18 months and three years after closing, and expanded its undrawn revolver to $7.7b through August 2031. It also has a pending $550m Blacklane acquisition and obtained control of Careem Technologies in July.

## Trait Assessment

### Earnings Quality — 0.75 / 0.84

Uber has now crossed into real profitability, but the record is still short. Revenue grew from $31.9b in 2022 to $52.0b in 2025, and Q2 2026 operating income rose 30% to $1.9b. Free cash flow is doing the important confirming work: Q2 FCF rose 13% to $2.8b and trailing-12-month FCF exceeded $10b. Receivables have stayed around 7-9% of revenue in the Yahoo-derived statements, not a warning sign.

The mark-down is for noise and history. GAAP earnings include volatile unrealized gains/losses on equity stakes—Q2 included a $1.6b pre-tax revaluation benefit after Q1's $1.5b loss—and the company only recently became consistently profitable. To firm this up, revisit a full-cycle owner-earnings bridge once several more profitable years are available.

### Free Cash Flow Generation — 0.90 / 0.90

This is the strongest cash-cow trait. Uber generated $9.8b FCF in FY2025, up 42% year over year, then produced $5.1b in the first half of 2026 and more than $10b over the trailing 12 months. Q2 FCF rose 13% to $2.8b, and capex remains tiny relative to cash generation. Yahoo-derived annual data shows FCF margins rising from 1.2% in 2022 to 9.0% in 2023, 15.7% in 2024 and 18.8% in 2025.

The caveat is stock-based compensation: SBC was about $1.8b in FY2025, roughly 19% of FCF, so true owner cash is lower than headline FCF unless buybacks more than offset dilution. The next update should check whether net share count continues falling.

### Fundamental Stability — 0.80 / 0.84

The operating base is becoming more stable. Q2 2026 extended the record with 208m MAPCs, 3.87b Trips, $58.0b Gross Bookings and $2.8b FCF. Mobility and Delivery are diversified across countries and use cases, while membership and cross-platform behavior deepen habit. The company also has enough cash generation to absorb normal competitive and regulatory friction.

The score is not higher because rideshare and delivery remain exposed to local regulation, labour rules, insurance inflation, driver/courier supply, discretionary consumer demand and city/airport concentration. Freight is also cyclical, though not thesis-central.

### Returns on Capital — 0.66 / 0.75

Returns are improving fast but are not yet elite on a long record. Yahoo-derived ROIC was roughly 13.1% in 2025, 11.8% in 2024 and 12.9% in 2023 after a deeply negative 2022. Incremental economics look much better than the average legacy base: revenue, operating income and FCF are all scaling faster than invested capital as the platform matures.

Interpretation: this is a marketplace with high incremental returns, but reported ROIC is still held down by acquisition goodwill, investment marks, historical losses and a business that only recently reached operating leverage. A clean ex-goodwill ROIC and segment-level capital allocation would firm up confidence.

### Pricing Power — 0.62 / 0.62

Uber has some pricing power through marketplace liquidity, brand, routing/pricing algorithms, membership, ads and the breadth of products. In Q2, Mobility and Delivery bookings grew 20% and 25% respectively in constant currency, while segment operating income grew 28% and 38%. The platform’s own disclosure that cross-platform users generate over 3x the Gross Bookings of single-offering users is good evidence that bundled utility matters.

But this is not classic untouchable pricing power. Uber explicitly warns that incentives, discounts, service-fee changes, driver supply constraints and local competition can pressure economics, and regulation can force model changes. Business-model changes cut Q2 reported revenue growth by eight percentage points. The score is therefore solid, not wonderful. Better evidence would be multi-year take-rate/margin stability by geography and product after normalising regulatory accounting changes.

### Reinvestment Runway — 0.78 / 0.70

The runway is credible. Uber operates in massive, still-fragmented transport, local commerce and logistics pools, with only about one in five eligible consumers active monthly across both Mobility and Delivery according to the 2025 10-K. Uber One reached 46m members at year-end 2025, Delivery continues to add grocery/retail and advertising, and the company is investing in AV platform growth. Consensus expects revenue of about $58.2b in 2026 and $67.1b in 2027, implying double-digit growth from the 2025 base.

The limitation is that some expansion requires continued incentives, regulatory navigation and product execution. Autonomous vehicles are also both opportunity and threat. Confidence is capped until we see how AV economics accrue between Uber, fleet owners and technology partners.

### Capital Allocation — 0.58 / 0.90

The operating cash return remains good: Uber generated $5.1b of FCF and repurchased $3.5b of stock in the first half of 2026, while Q2 diluted weighted-average shares fell 3.5% year over year to 2.05b. But the allocation story is no longer mainly buybacks. Uber spent $2.3b increasing its Delivery Hero stake to 24.99%, committed $1.6b to Delivery Hero-linked total-return swaps, and agreed to acquire the remaining shares at an implied $14.8b equity value for the whole company. The August financing replaces €4.0b of the €14.2b bridge with committed term debt, reducing bridge exposure but confirming that acquisition funding will compete with buybacks and other transactions. Blacklane ($550m) and the July purchase of control in Careem Technologies add further execution demands.

Delivery Hero is strategically coherent with Uber's local-commerce network, but the price, financing and integration outcomes are not yet auditable and the transaction reverses the clean cash-cow narrative. Q4 2025 buybacks also occurred around $84-95 per share, above the current quote used here, and SBC remains meaningful at roughly $1.8b per year. The score falls into the mixed band until management demonstrates per-share accretion, debt reduction and integration discipline.

### Conservative Debt — 0.70 / 0.88

The current balance sheet remains serviceable but is no longer plainly conservative. Uber ended Q2 2026 with $5.4b of unrestricted cash and short-term investments against $12.7b of debt, including a $2.0b term loan due in December 2026. More than $10b of trailing FCF, a new $7.7b revolver through August 2031 and laddered senior notes keep near-term resilience high. The revolver was undrawn at signing apart from about $324m of transitioned letters of credit.

The pending Delivery Hero offer materially changes the forward posture. On August 6 Uber replaced €4.0b of the bridge with unsecured term-loan tranches maturing 18 months and three years after the acquisition closes; their EURIBOR spreads and commitment fees vary with Uber's debt rating, and both the term loans and revolver require at least 3.0× adjusted EBITDA-to-interest coverage. The shorter maturity limits permanent bridge risk but creates a visible refinancing/deleveraging clock. Existing convertibles/exchangeables, letters of credit, insurance liabilities and regulatory/legal exposures also sit ahead of equity. Solvency risk remains low given cash generation, but leverage could constrain buybacks and strategic optionality until the acquisition debt is reduced.

### Key-Person Risk — 0.72 / 0.62

Dara Khosrowshahi remains important, but Uber no longer looks like a single-founder/key-person story. The operating model is institutionalised across Mobility, Delivery and Freight; COO Andrew Macdonald and new CFO Balaji Krishnamurthy were promoted from inside, suggesting succession depth. The score is not higher because Khosrowshahi’s turnaround credibility and capital-allocation reset are part of the current thesis.

### Incentive Alignment — 0.72 / 0.78

The 2026 proxy is mostly encouraging: 96% of CEO target direct compensation was variable/at risk; more than 77.5% of the CEO’s target annual equity opportunity is tied to TSR and performance via options/PRSUs; 2025 incentives included Gross Bookings, Adjusted EBITDA, Adjusted EBITDA less SBC, AV platform growth, membership, safety, EV miles and driver/courier experience; and the CEO has a 10x base-salary stock ownership guideline. Including SBC in the bonus metric is a useful shareholder-friendly improvement.

The mark-down is that the metric basket is broad and can reward size as well as per-share value. Ownership is also institution-heavy and insider ownership is low by founder-led standards.

### Strategy fit and why the others lost

The scorer produced a genuine near-tie: Cash Cow was the narrow metric winner, with Rule Breakers and Wonderful-and-Fair close behind. I am leaving the canonical strategy as Cash Cow because the current investable case is no longer “will this disruptor survive?”; it is “how much durable FCF does this scaled platform return while still growing?” That said, this is an unusual cash cow: it still has a real reinvestment runway and no dividend, so the cash return is buyback-led rather than income-led.

Rule Breakers remains a valid secondary lens because Uber is still growing bookings/users in huge markets and has AV/platform optionality. Wonderful-and-Fair also fits as a profitable compounder, but its score is held back by non-elite reported ROIC and messy pricing-power evidence. Freeroll does not fit: there is no hard asset/NAV floor or time-bound catalyst.

## Overall

Uber is now a high-quality global marketplace cash generator at a price that looks reasonable rather than obviously cheap. Q2 strengthened the operating case: constant-currency bookings rose 22%, operating income rose 30%, FCF rose 13%, and diluted shares fell 3.5%. The Delivery Hero takeover creates potentially useful scale in local commerce but adds a large debt-funded integration bet to a thesis that had been becoming simpler. The main detractors are competition and regulation, the short history of clean profitability, investment noise in GAAP earnings, forward acquisition leverage, and uncertainty over how AV value gets split.

My valuation-aware score is 0.68: operating momentum is attractive, but the Delivery Hero price, financing and integration economics must now work for per-share value. The moat/pricing-power evidence remains messier than a classic wonderful business, and the cash-cow frame is less dependable while cash and debt are being redirected from buybacks to M&A.

## Thesis Connections

- Platform compounding: Uber is a clear example of a two-sided marketplace where liquidity, consumer habit, advertising and membership can reinforce each other.
- AI/autonomy optionality: AV partnerships could lower supply constraints and expand trip volume, but may also shift economics toward fleet/AV partners. Treat as upside optionality, not base-case value.
- Cash-generation transition: this is a former hyper-growth story becoming a FCF compounder with buybacks.

## Watch

- Q3 2026 bookings growth against 18-22% constant-currency guidance and non-GAAP EPS of $0.84-$0.88.
- AV economics: look for disclosed unit economics, partner terms, city launches and whether AV trips expand Uber’s take or compress it.
- Take-rate and incentives: monitor whether Mobility/Delivery revenue margins hold after UK accounting/model changes and competitive incentives.
- Delivery Hero: offer acceptance and regulatory approvals; drawdown and pricing of the €4.0b term loans and remaining bridge; refinancing of the 18-month tranche; consolidated FCF/share accretion, integration milestones and post-close deleveraging.
- Blacklane and Careem Technologies: purchase economics, integration and whether multiple simultaneous transactions distract from the core.
- SBC versus buybacks: buybacks should reduce share count net of SBC once acquisition funding permits, not merely mask dilution.
- Regulatory/labour risk: driver classification, wage floors, airport rules, VAT/tax treatment and marketplace safety liabilities.
