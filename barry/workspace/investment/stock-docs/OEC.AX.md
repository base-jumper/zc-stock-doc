# OEC.AX — Orbital Corporation Limited

## Business Overview

Orbital Corporation designs and manufactures integrated propulsion systems and flight-critical components for Group 2 and Group 3 tactical UAVs. Its core products are heavy-fuel engines and related engine-management/power systems used in long-endurance military and commercial UAV platforms. The company operates from Balcatta, Western Australia, with US in-service support capability.

Revenue currently comes from a mix of engine-system sales, in-service support and engineering development work. FY25 operational revenue was $8.2m, down from the prior year, with $6.9m from heavy-fuel engine exports to core customers in the US, Singapore and Vietnam and $1.3m from engineering development work. H1 FY26 revenue was $4.9m, broadly flat on the prior corresponding period, made up of $2.9m engine/system and support revenue and $1.9m engineering services revenue.

This is still a very small, lumpy, pre-scale defence supplier. The upside case is that Orbital's heavy-fuel propulsion niche becomes strategically valuable as defence and commercial UAV demand shifts toward longer endurance, higher payload and BVLOS-capable platforms. The downside is that the current business has not yet shown durable profitable scale, customer/program timing is outside Orbital's control, and liquidity is increasingly dependent on shareholder facilities.

## Trait Assessment

### Right Place and Time — 0.80 / 0.75

The industry setup is attractive. Orbital serves Group 2/3 UAVs, a segment supported by structural defence demand, sovereign drone capability build-outs, increased payload requirements, longer endurance missions and BVLOS commercial regulation. In the March 2026 quarter, Orbital estimated US Group 2/3 military propulsion TAM at US$1.0–2.0b over the five years to CY2032, and US commercial Group 2/3 propulsion TAM at US$1.5–2.2b over the same period.

The company is also positioned around heavy-fuel engines, which are relevant for military logistics/safety and longer-endurance platforms. Management cites US procurement and regulatory shifts, Australian BVLOS reforms and European/NATO opportunities as market tailwinds.

Interpretation: the wave is real, but the market is still procurement-driven and timing is messy. US Army Group 2/3 procurement was paused in 2025 while priorities shifted after Ukraine-war lessons, which is a reminder that defence demand can be structurally attractive and still commercially stop-start.

To firm up: independent Group 2/3 UAV procurement forecasts, competitor map for heavy-fuel propulsion, and contract-level evidence that these TAMs convert into addressable engine orders for Orbital.

### Top Dog — 0.45 / 0.60

Orbital is a credible niche supplier, not yet a proven top dog. Evidence in its favour: more than 1.0m active flight hours over eight years with the US Navy through ScanEagle and Aerosonde 4.7 platforms; active Tier 1 defence-customer relationships including Boeing Insitu and Textron; four propulsion-system models in production or production-ready status; and 12 active customers across 5 geographic regions as of March 2026.

But the company is tiny: FY25 revenue was only $8.4m, H1 FY26 revenue $4.9m, and March-quarter customer receipts $1.6m. Orbital may be technically strong in a specialised niche, but there is not enough evidence that it captures a dominant share of the broader Group 2/3 propulsion market or that platform OEMs are locked into its products at scale.

To firm up: disclosed win rates, market share in Group 2/3 heavy-fuel engines, contract values by platform, and evidence that OEMs choose Orbital over named competitors for new programs.

### Sustainable Competitive Advantage — 0.60 / 0.65

Orbital has plausible technical advantages: proprietary heavy-fuel direct-injection and engine-management technology, 30 patents covering engine hardware and control systems, in-service operational hours, and the qualification burden involved in flight-critical defence systems. Once an engine is integrated into a UAV platform, switching suppliers is not trivial.

The weakness is that the moat is not yet translating into high, stable economics. Revenue remains small and lumpy, and several product extensions — 350HFE, ICE-electric hybrid systems, Generation 2 power management and a planned 500cc HFE — are still being commercialised. The moat is therefore credible but not yet proven as a durable profit pool.

To firm up: gross margin by mature production program, evidence of recurring spares/support revenue, patent scope review, and competitive displacement history.

### Durable Growth — 0.50 / 0.70

The growth evidence is mixed but improving at the order level. Orbital broadened from a single product/customer setup in 2021 to 12 active customers, 11 products and 5 regions by March 2026. At 31 July it had A$3.7m of orders scheduled for Q1 FY27 delivery, including the first 20 50HFE systems under its UAE distribution agreement. It also added AATI as a US commercial customer, with orders for eight 150HFE engines and associated work, and expanded its Dynamatic/Cheel participation in India.

Financially, the business has not yet inflected. FY25 customer receipts were A$8.28m, while June-quarter receipts fell to A$1.29m and net operating cash outflow widened to A$0.79m. Orbital also spent A$1.32m on intellectual property during the quarter, so the order book has yet to demonstrate repeatable self-funded growth.

Interpretation: the operating pipeline is interesting, but durable growth is not yet demonstrated in reported revenue or cash flow. This score stays below the clear-pass band until orders convert into repeatable revenue and gross profit.

To firm up: FY27 results showing conversion of the A$3.7m Q1 order schedule into revenue and cash receipts, mature program gross margins, and backlog coverage beyond the current quarter.

### Organic Growth — 0.85 / 0.75

Growth is overwhelmingly organic. Orbital's product expansion is internally developed: 50cc, 75cc, 150cc, 350cc and planned 500cc engines, ICE-electric hybrid systems, and power-management hardware. I found no material acquisition-driven growth in the recent reporting set.

The caveat is that organic does not automatically mean high quality. Internally funded development consumes cash, and some opportunities are still pre-commercial.

### Great Management & Smart Backing — 0.60 / 0.65

Stephen Pearce was appointed CEO effective 30 June 2025, with the board citing defence UAV experience and prior commercialisation/manufacturing transition experience. His package included $400k fixed remuneration, STI up to 30% of TFR and 500k options. The appointment completed a broader management strengthening process alongside CFO and engineering leadership hires.

The largest shareholders, UIL Limited and Boneyard Investments, have supported the company through underwriting/participation in capital raisings and an unsecured A$3.5m working-capital facility. A$2.0m had been drawn by 30 June, leaving A$1.5m available. That is useful backing, but also confirms continued dependence on external support.

Interpretation: management quality is plausible and newly better matched to the commercialisation challenge, but still unproven. The hard test is turning technical credibility into scaled production contracts without repeat dilution.

To firm up: CEO and director ownership after recent raises, exact STI/LTI KPI design, FY27 execution against the order book, and customer conversion under the new team.

### Incentive Alignment — 0.50 / 0.55

The incentive picture is mixed. CEO remuneration includes options, and the company uses equity incentives, which can align management with shareholders if grants are sensibly struck and tied to value creation. The CEO's STI is based on annual KPI objectives, but the public appointment announcement did not disclose the detailed metrics. FY25 remuneration material says incentives may measure market capitalisation or share price, which can align with shareholders but can also reward sentiment rather than per-share intrinsic value.

Large shareholder support is a positive, but there is not enough evidence of management personally buying meaningful stock with their own capital. The repeated need for equity/facility support also means dilution discipline matters.

To firm up: full FY26 remuneration report, insider holdings and buying/selling, STI/LTI hurdle detail, and dilution from options/equity grants.

### Customer Devotion — 0.55 / 0.55

Customer evidence is positive but indirect. More than 1.0m flight hours with US Navy platforms and continuing work with Boeing Insitu/Textron suggest the product is trusted in demanding use cases. March 2026 updates showed initial orders or delivery activity with Textron, ST Engineering, Dynamatic/Dynauton, a UAE sovereign customer, Freespace Operations and other customers.

But this is defence/industrial procurement, not a product with clean NPS, retention or expansion metrics. We can infer technical trust from repeat programs and flight hours, but not customer devotion in the usual sense.

To firm up: renewal/support revenue, repeat-order rates, customer concentration, named customer testimonials tied to performance, and program-level expansion.

**Why other strategies lost:** Freeroll loses because there is no credible valuation floor near the market value: H1 FY26 net tangible assets were only 1.81c/share and June cash was A$1.68m, partly funded by A$2.0m drawn shareholder debt. Wonderful-and-fair loses outright because Orbital has no demonstrated consistent earnings quality: the company remains loss-making and the H1 auditor flagged material uncertainty related to going concern.

## Overall

OEC is an interesting defence-drone picks-and-shovels microcap, but it is not yet a high-quality investment. The setup has genuine appeal: heavy-fuel UAV propulsion is strategically relevant, the product has real flight history, the customer list is widening, and the opportunity pipeline is meaningful relative to the company's size.

The problem is proof and survivability. Orbital remains sub-scale, loss-making, cash-constrained and dependent on uncertain program timing. June cash was A$1.68m after drawing A$2.0m of its shareholder facility, leaving A$1.5m undrawn. The reported 4.0 quarters of funding uses operating burn only; including the quarter's A$1.32m intellectual-property investment, cash plus undrawn facilities covered only about 1.5 June-like quarters. The A$3.7m Q1 FY27 order schedule may improve receipts, so this does not yet trigger the universal solvency gate, but dilution/liquidity risk is central to the thesis.

Current view: **watch / speculative only**. I would not treat OEC as a buy-quality Rule Breaker until the A$3.7m Q1 FY27 order schedule converts into revenue and cash, investment burn moderates, and the company proves it can fund growth without repeatedly leaning on shareholders.

## Valuation

Yahoo's latest available close was A$0.14 when checked on 3 August 2026, with a quoted market capitalisation of about A$25.1m.

This is hard to underwrite with an earnings/FCF exit-multiple model because current earnings and FCF are negative. FY25 revenue was A$8.4m and loss after tax was A$4.3m; H1 FY26 revenue was A$4.9m and loss after tax was A$1.5m. The quoted market cap is roughly 3.0x FY25 sales and 2.6x annualised H1 FY26 sales. That may be cheap if the A$43m pipeline converts into high-margin production/support revenue, but it is expensive relative to current proof and the balance-sheet risk.

Downside support is weak. H1 FY26 NTA was 1.81c/share, far below the A$0.14 reference price, and June cash fell to A$1.68m after A$2.0m of shareholder borrowings. The real valuation question is therefore binary-ish: can Orbital convert its customer/program pipeline into scaled production before liquidity/dilution overwhelms shareholders?

I am not recording an exit-multiple ROI yet because there is no defensible positive current per-share earnings or FCF anchor. The next update should add a valuation block only if Orbital provides enough FY27 revenue/gross-margin visibility to normalise earnings or owner earnings.

## Watch

- Q1 FY27 order conversion: did the A$3.7m scheduled deliveries, including 20 UAE systems, ship and convert into cash at acceptable gross margin?
- The A$43m pipeline: named contract wins, conversion rate, and whether any single award materially changes backlog.
- Liquidity: cash balance, the remaining A$1.5m shareholder facility, development spend, and any equity raise or further shareholder debt.
- Cash burn after headcount reduction from 54 to 42 and product-cost reductions.
- Textron/Aerosonde, Boeing Insitu/ScanEagle, ST Engineering Veloce, Dynamatic/Cheel, UAE LTA and Freespace/Callisto milestones.
- Whether in-service support and Power-by-the-Hour become recurring revenue rather than one-off product sales.

- Any ASX disclosure that removes or worsens the going-concern uncertainty.
