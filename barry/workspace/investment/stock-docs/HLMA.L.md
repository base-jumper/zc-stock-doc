# HLMA.L — Halma plc

## Business Overview

Halma is a FTSE 100 group of decentralised life-safety and environmental/healthcare technology businesses. It buys and builds niche companies aligned to three long-term themes: Safety (37% of FY26 revenue), Environmental & Analysis (40%), and Healthcare (23%). Revenue comes from specialist products used to protect people/assets, monitor environmental and scientific conditions, and improve healthcare outcomes.

FY26 was a very strong year: revenue rose 14.9% to £2.58bn and adjusted EBIT rose 22.2% to £594.5m. Organic revenue growth was 16.6% (16.2% excluding a one-off Nuvonic transaction), helped materially by the Avo Photonics business; Halma says photonics contributed roughly eight percentage points of group organic revenue growth and expects roughly five points in FY27. This is still a classic Halma setup: diversified niche industrial/healthcare tech, decentralised execution, disciplined M&A, high cash conversion, and a progressive dividend.

## Trait Assessment

### Earnings Quality / Consistent, Predictable Profits — 0.90 / confidence 0.86

Halma has the earnings record we want from a wonderful-and-fair compounder. FY26 was the 23rd consecutive year of adjusted profit growth, and statutory net income has grown in every Yahoo-covered year: £234.5m in FY23, £268.8m in FY24, £296.4m in FY25, and £372.3m in FY26. Earnings are also cash-backed: free cash flow was £409.6m in FY26 and cumulative FCF/net income over FY23–FY26 was about 1.18x. Receivables/revenue was stable-to-lower, 17.4% in FY23 to 16.5% in FY26, which does not scream aggressive accruals.

The main caveat is that Halma reports heavily on adjusted profit, and acquisition-related amortisation is a recurring feature of the model. I do not treat that as disqualifying because statutory profit and FCF are both rising, but next update should re-check the gap between adjusted and statutory results rather than blindly accepting the adjusted series.

### High, Durable Returns on Capital — 0.72 / confidence 0.82

Returns are good and improving, though not absurdly high on a goodwill-inclusive basis. Halma reported adjusted ROTIC of 16.2% in FY26, within its 12–17% target range and materially above its stated WACC of 10.2%. Yahoo-derived ROIC rose from 10.9% in FY23 to 13.4% in FY26, while ROCE rose from 12.6% to 16.5%. Incremental ROIC from the helper script was about 21.6%, which supports the thesis that recent capital is still being deployed productively.

The constraint on the score is goodwill: goodwill was roughly 51% of invested capital in FY26, reflecting the acquisition-heavy model. Returns are likely much higher on tangible operating capital, but including goodwill is the honest test of whether M&A prices are creating value. On that basis Halma is solidly value-creating, not a flawless 25%+ ROIC machine.

### Pricing Power / Durable Moat — 0.74 / confidence 0.76

The moat is a portfolio of niche technical positions rather than one dominant brand. Evidence is decent: gross margin has stayed close to 50%, adjusted EBIT margin reached 23.0%, and FY26 organic growth included average price increases of 1–2% while revenue and profit still grew across all three sectors. The business also benefits from regulated/critical applications where reliability matters more than lowest price.

The weaker point is customer concentration inside the high-growth photonics business: one hyperscaler relationship accounted for 20% of group revenue in FY26, up from 15%. That relationship is a sign of technical advantage and customer trust, but also makes this part of the moat more concentrated than the headline diversified-group story suggests.

### Reinvestment Runway — 0.82 / confidence 0.78

Halma has a credible long runway. Its markets — safety, environmental monitoring, analytical/photonics technology, and healthcare — are tied to structural drivers: infrastructure safety, pollution/resource monitoring, scientific analysis, chronic disease, and ageing populations. The company also has repeatable reinvestment routes: R&D, capacity investment, leadership development, and bolt-on M&A.

FY26 reinforces the point: Halma invested £123m in R&D (4.7% of revenue), £56m in capex, and £447m in five acquisitions, with two further bolt-ons after year-end. The concern is that FY26 growth was unusually boosted by photonics. Avo’s hyperscaler-linked growth is attractive, but its pace and customer concentration mean it should not be extrapolated forever. The score is high because the runway exists; confidence is slightly lower because the current growth mix is not yet fully normalised.

### Capital Allocation — 0.82 / confidence 0.84

The capital allocation pattern is rational: organic growth first, value-enhancing acquisitions second, regular dividends third. FY26 capital allocation matched that hierarchy: R&D and capex increased, acquisitions were active, leverage stayed controlled, and the dividend rose for the 47th consecutive year by at least 5%. The dividend is well covered; FY26 cash dividends of £89.7m were covered about 4.6x by FCF.

M&A is the key judgement call. Halma spent £1.12bn on acquisitions across FY23–FY26, and goodwill sits around 40% of assets. The record looks good — rising margins, rising ROTIC, stable share count, no obvious balance-sheet stress — but acquisition price discipline must remain the watch item. Serial acquirers can look wonderful until one cycle of overpayment catches up.

### Conservative Debt / Financial Strength — 0.90 / confidence 0.86

The balance sheet is conservatively financed for a diversified industrial technology group. Reported net debt/adjusted EBITDA was 1.16x at FY26 year-end, comfortably within the company’s operating range of up to 2x. The helper script’s annual series showed net debt/EBITDA around 1.0x and EBIT interest cover of 18.2x in FY26. Current and quick ratios were also healthy at 2.14x and 1.41x.

Liquidity looks strong: Halma disclosed committed facilities above £1.2bn. The remaining evidence gap is the full maturity ladder and fixed/floating split; those should be checked if leverage rises or acquisition pace accelerates.

### Key-Person Risk — 0.84 / confidence 0.76

Halma is deliberately designed to reduce key-person risk. The decentralised model pushes ownership to company boards and managing directors, while the group invests heavily in leadership development and succession. In FY26, 20 leaders were promoted onto company boards and nearly 300 leaders participated in development programmes. Sector leadership transitions were also handled through internal promotions.

The score is not 1.0 because the model still depends on maintaining a culture of disciplined decentralisation and acquisition judgement. Losing one executive should not break the business; losing the cultural machinery over time would.

### Incentive Alignment — 0.80 / confidence 0.82

Executive incentives are broadly pointed at owner outcomes. FY26 annual bonus was driven 95% by Economic Value Added and 5% by DEI; long-term awards used adjusted EPS growth and adjusted ROTIC. EVA and ROTIC are better than plain revenue growth or adjusted EBITDA because they make capital efficiency matter. Bonus deferral, share awards, shareholding requirements, and malus/clawback also help.

The caveat is the reliance on adjusted measures, especially for an acquisitive group. Adjusted EPS and adjusted ROTIC are sensible but require scrutiny around acquisition costs, amortisation, and one-offs. Overall, the system is aligned enough to support the thesis.

**Why other strategies lost:** Cash-cow scores similarly well mechanically because Halma is profitable, cash generative, and stable, but it is not primarily a harvest-and-distribute story: the dividend yield is below 1%, and management is actively reinvesting in R&D, capacity, talent, and acquisitions. Freeroll fails because there is no hard valuation floor or time-bound catalyst; at roughly 29x current earnings it is the opposite of a cheap asymmetric setup. Rule-breakers is directionally relevant because photonics is growing fast, but Halma is a proven diversified compounder, not an early-stage winner-takes-most disruptor.

## Overall

Halma is a high-quality compounder with the right ingredients: consistent profit growth, strong cash conversion, disciplined decentralised M&A, robust balance sheet, and exposure to durable safety/environmental/healthcare demand. It fits wonderful-and-fair best.

The problem is valuation. At 3,640p, the stock trades at roughly 29x my current earnings-power estimate and about 26x FY27 consensus EPS. That is not obviously irrational for Halma’s quality, but it leaves little margin for disappointment if photonics normalises faster, the hyperscaler relationship weakens, or acquisition returns fade.

## Thesis Connections

Fits the “wonderful business at a fair price” bucket: durable niche leadership, decentralised culture, acquisition flywheel, and long-term structural demand. It is especially relevant to themes around safety-critical infrastructure, environmental measurement, healthcare tools, and data-centre photonics.

## Watch

- Photonics concentration: track Avo Photonics growth, hyperscaler exposure, and whether FY27 premium growth really lands around five percentage points of group organic revenue growth.
- Acquisition discipline: monitor acquisition spend, goodwill/assets, impairments, and ROTIC versus WACC after the FY26 record M&A year.
- Adjusted-vs-statutory gap: recurring acquisition amortisation and “one-offs” should not obscure real owner earnings.
- Organic growth excluding photonics: confirm the wider group remains a high-single-digit organic grower after the current photonics surge normalises.
- Leverage: keep net debt/adjusted EBITDA under 2x unless there is a clearly exceptional deal.
- Revisit valuation if price drops below ~3,000p or if current earnings power moves above £1.35/share without multiple expansion.
