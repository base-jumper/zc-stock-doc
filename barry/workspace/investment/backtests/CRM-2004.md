### Salesforce entry-price and capital structure inputs

- **Entry price:** `$4.226873397827148` per share, Yahoo historical close for CRM on 2004-06-23. This is split-adjusted. The IPO prospectus price was `$11.00`; Yahoo’s historical series is used only for the requested split-adjusted first-day close.
- **Shares outstanding after offering:** `101,256,880` common shares, from 424B1 capitalization/share-count text: 91,256,880 shares outstanding as of 2004-04-30 after preferred conversion plus 10,000,000 IPO shares. Source: Salesforce 424B1 filed 2004-06-23.
- **Options outstanding:** `16,422,047` shares issuable on options as of 2004-04-30, exercise prices `$0.03–$8.00`; final 424B1 gives weighted-average exercise price `$3.22`. Source: Salesforce 424B1 filed 2004-06-23.
- **Warrants outstanding:** `1,299,496` shares issuable on warrants as of 2004-04-30, exercise prices `$1.10–$3.89`, weighted-average exercise price `$2.43`. Source: Salesforce 424B1 filed 2004-06-23.
- **Reserved/future option-pool shares:** `693,818` remaining under the 1999 plan as of 2004-04-30, plus newly authorized 2004 plans: 4,000,000 Equity Incentive Plan shares, 1,000,000 Outside Directors Stock Plan shares, and 1,000,000 Employee Stock Purchase Plan shares. Source: Salesforce 424B1 filed 2004-06-23.
- **Valuation share base used:** `486.8m` split-adjusted shares. Construction: fully diluted pre-split base of roughly `119.67m` = 101.257m post-offering basic + 16.422m options + 1.299m warrants + 0.694m remaining 1999 option reserve; multiplied by the approximate Yahoo split-adjustment factor implied by the first-day close series. I did **not** add the entire future 2004 plan authorization to the starting share base because the valuation model handles future employee issuance through dilution.
- **Net cash/debt at IPO:** Salesforce 424B1 capitalization table shows pro forma as-adjusted cash, cash equivalents and short-term marketable securities of `$142.033m` after the offering, and capital lease obligations of `$80k`; net cash approximately `$141.95m`. Source: Salesforce 424B1 filed 2004-06-23. In the script, I set `net-debt=0` for the terminal TAM-capture cases because the script’s `net-debt` parameter is terminal net debt, not IPO balance-sheet cash; IPO cash is recorded here as source evidence and implicitly supports lower failure/dilution risk.

### Salesforce market-size inputs available in the prospectus

- Salesforce described itself as providing comprehensive CRM services delivered through a web browser. Source: Salesforce 424B1 filed 2004-06-23.
- **On-demand application services market:** projected by IDC to grow from `$425m` in 2002 to `$2.6b` in 2007, a 44% CAGR. Source cited in Salesforce prospectus: IDC, May 2003 report.
- **CRM applications market:** approximately `$7.1b` in 2002. Source cited in Salesforce prospectus: IDC, July 2003 report.
- **Base 2012 TAM used:** `$12.0b`, an analyst assumption from 2004 facts: starting from IDC’s `$7.1b` 2002 CRM applications market and applying roughly 5–6% annual growth to 2012. I deliberately did **not** apply the 44% on-demand-app-services CAGR to the whole CRM market because that IDC figure sized a smaller delivery-model category, not all CRM software.
- **Blue-sky 2012 TAM override:** `$16.1b`, based on IDC’s on-demand application services projection of `$2.6b` in 2007 extended five more years at the same 44% CAGR (`2.6b × 1.44^5 ≈ 16.1b`). This is aggressive, but still argued from a pre-IPO source: Salesforce’s prospectus said on-demand services could replace purchased software with subscriptions to a wide range of application services, not just CRM. This is the “SaaS reaches beyond CRM” case.

### Role-model and scenario assumptions

#### It-works role model: Siebel Systems (SEBL)

- **Why it fits as of 2004:** Siebel was the obvious public CRM category leader and mature CRM software anchor; Salesforce’s prospectus named the CRM applications market and competed against larger incumbent application vendors.
- **Siebel 2003 revenue:** `$1.354b`, from Siebel 2003 Form 10-K selected financial data.
- **Siebel own-market capture:** `~19%`, calculated as Siebel 2003 revenue `$1.354b` divided by IDC’s CRM applications market `$7.1b` in 2002. Imperfect timing, but it keeps capture measured against the role model’s **own** CRM market, not Salesforce’s later market.
- **Mature EBIT margin:** `17%`, using Siebel’s 2001 operating income `$357.9m` on revenue `$2.085b` from the 2003 10-K selected financial history. I used the pre-downturn mature software margin rather than 2003’s depressed negative margin, because the terminal case asks what Salesforce could earn if the model worked at scale.
- **Exit multiple:** `12× EBIT`, a deliberately rounded mature-software EV/EBIT assumption using Siebel as the qualitative anchor. This is weaker than the other inputs because I did not have a clean SEBL Yahoo historical price series available through `yfin`; I therefore kept the multiple moderate and record this as a warning-grade assumption.

#### Niche-survivor scenario

- **Role model / reference class:** pre-2004 hosted CRM/ASP peers such as UpShot or Salesnet: viable enough to be bought or continue serving a niche, but not category-defining.
- **Inputs:** 2% of base CRM TAM, 5% EBIT margin, 8× EBIT exit multiple, 7% annual dilution. This represents a small surviving vendor with weak operating leverage and shareholder leakage.

#### Failure scenario

- **Terminal equity:** `$50m`, approximating residual cash/IP/acqui-hire value after a failed growth push. This is deliberately low relative to IPO market cap because the failure path likely consumes cash before sale or liquidation.

#### Blue-sky scenario

- **Role-model ceiling:** Oracle/SAP as already-mature enterprise software giants by 2004, used as the ceiling for what “SaaS expands beyond CRM” could mean. The point is not that Salesforce was Oracle in 2004, but that the largest realistic outcome had to be capped at a real enterprise-software scale already observable by then.
- **Inputs:** `$16.1b` TAM override, 25% capture, 30% EBIT margin, 18× EBIT exit multiple, 3% annual dilution. This intentionally combines a larger market boundary and higher capture, so it must pass the scenario-tam tail warning; it did not fully pass, noted below.

### Probability reasoning

Salesforce was not pre-revenue: it had commercial traction, rapid revenue growth, and had recently turned marginally profitable. However, it was still early, concentrated in on-demand CRM, selling against much larger incumbents, and depended on enterprises accepting a hosted subscription application model. I used the **profitable growth-stage** scenario-tam starting band, then adjusted down for category/platform uncertainty:

- Profitable growth-stage starting band: failure 15–35%, niche 30–45%, it-works 20–35%, blue-sky 3–8%.
- Chosen probabilities: **failure 34% residual, niche-survivor 40%, it-works 22%, blue-sky 4%**.
- Stage-gate decomposition for `it-works`:
  - hosted CRM technically proven and usable in production: high, roughly 75%;
  - enterprises keep adopting subscriptions for core front-office workflows: roughly 60%;
  - Salesforce scales sales/product fast enough to reach multi-billion revenue before incumbents copy or bundle: roughly 55%;
  - achieves mature software profitability without infrastructure/support costs overwhelming the model: roughly 70%.
  - Product: `0.75 × 0.60 × 0.55 × 0.70 ≈ 17%`; I lifted to **22%** because Salesforce already had strong commercial traction and marginal profitability at IPO.
- Blue-sky probability of **4%** sits inside the 3–8% profitable-growth band but below the midpoint: the prospectus-supported “wide range of application services” possibility existed, but broad SaaS platform dominance was not yet proved in 2004.
- Deviation notes: failure at **34%** is near the top of the profitable-growth band because switching enterprises from licensed/on-premise software to hosted subscription apps was still a major adoption risk in 2004, and incumbents had far more resources.

### Dilution reasoning

- Starting fully diluted base includes IPO shares, existing options, warrants, and remaining 1999 option reserve, but excludes the entire future plan authorization.
- Script dilution path: **3% per year** for it-works and blue-sky; **7% per year** for niche-survivor. Basis: Salesforce had a large option pool and annual evergreen-like plan increases in the 2004 plans, but IPO cash and marginal profitability reduced the need for distressed financing in successful scenarios.
- The 424B1 2004 Equity Incentive Plan allowed annual increases through 2013: 5% of then-outstanding shares or 5m in 2005–2006; 4% or 4m in 2007–2008; and 3.5% or 3.5m in 2009–2013, subject to board reduction. This supports ongoing dilution even without capital raises.

- **CRM market share:** Gartner’s 2013 CRM market-share release reported the worldwide CRM software market at roughly `$18b` in 2012 and Salesforce as the market-share leader at about 14% by vendor share. ### Cohort calibration

- **Siebel actual ending:** Siebel did not remain an independent compounding CRM winner. Oracle announced/acquired Siebel in 2005/2006 for roughly `$5.85b`, turning the obvious 2004 role model into an acquired incumbent rather than a long-run standalone anchor. That matters: the “mature CRM leader” reference class was much less durable than the Salesforce outcome.
- **A 2004-era CRM/SaaS peer that stalled / became niche:** Salesnet/UpShot-style hosted CRM peers did not become category platforms. UpShot had already been absorbed by Siebel before Salesforce’s IPO; Salesnet was later absorbed into another CRM vendor rather than compounding independently. This supports the niche/failure scenarios existing, even though Salesforce escaped them.

### What was most wrong

1. **The valuation framework stopped at “mature CRM vendor,” but the market rewarded “emerging SaaS platform.”** Revenue in the it-works case was basically right, yet the predicted terminal equity was only `$4.65b` versus a realized June 2012 equity value around `$19b`. The miss was not “Salesforce sold more CRM than expected”; it was that investors paid for a much longer runway and platform expansion.
2. **Margins were directionally wrong but did not hurt the stock because the market ignored near-term GAAP EBIT.** The model expected 17% EBIT; Salesforce was still GAAP loss-making. Under a strict EBIT multiple this should have been disastrous, but the market valued growth, retention, SaaS economics, and optionality.
3. **TAM was too narrow.** Even CRM alone appears to have grown to around `$18b`, above both the base `$12b` case and the `$16.1b` on-demand-app blue-sky boundary. The prospectus contained a clue — “wide range of application services” — but the Phase 1 model did not give it enough room.
4. **Scenario probabilities were honest but too outside-view-heavy.** A 4% blue-sky probability was defensible from 2004 base rates, but the company’s actual early evidence — leadership in on-demand CRM, rapid growth, marginal profitability, and strong IPO cash position — deserved either a higher blue-sky probability or a broader blue-sky valuation.

### Method lessons

- **For SaaS IPOs, year-8 may be too early for “mature margin × mature multiple.”** If the company is still reinvesting successfully at year 8, EV/EBIT will understate value. Scenario-tam needs a “still compounding / not mature yet” endpoint or a revenue-multiple bridge.
- **The blue-sky TAM override should explicitly test category expansion, not just the original named TAM.** Salesforce’s prospectus had enough pre-2004 language to justify a broader SaaS/application-services path, but the model constrained it too tightly.
- **Role-model capture can be misleading when the new model expands the market.** Siebel’s share of old CRM was the wrong ceiling for a cloud-native company creating/reshaping demand.
- **Tail-alpha warnings are useful but not sufficient.** The Phase 1 script warned that the blue-sky tail was fat; ex post, the right fix was not necessarily to lower the tail, but to better specify the platform scenario and its probability from stage gates.
- **Probability-weighted outputs can look bad even when the buy is right.** Scenario-tam correctly highlighted that most expected value sat in low-probability upside. But for power-law SaaS/platform names, the investment decision may depend on whether the blue-sky probability is 4%, 8%, or 12%, not on the modal outcome.

### Proposed doc/script improvements for review only

1. Add a **“not mature by horizon” terminal mode** to scenario-tam: value terminal revenue with an EV/revenue multiple when EBIT is intentionally suppressed by reinvestment, with a separate long-run margin sanity check.
2. Add a required **TAM-boundary audit**: original niche TAM, adjacent TAM, and platform TAM, each with source/date and an explicit “what has to be true” note.
3. Add a **role-model market-expansion check**: if the subject’s model can grow the category, do not use incumbent share mechanically as the capture ceiling.
4. Add a **probability sensitivity table** for blue-sky probability and TAM, because small changes in right-tail probability dominated this case.
5. Add a **script warning when terminal margin is high but the horizon company is still likely reinvesting** — i.e. “mature margin by year N may be inconsistent with hypergrowth.”
6. Add a **split-adjustment consistency note** in raw-mode examples: if price is split-adjusted, shares must be split-adjusted too.
