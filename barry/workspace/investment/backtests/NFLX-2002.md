## Phase 1 — Predict, pretending date is 2002-05-23

### Source boundary

Only pre-June-2002 source facts are used in this section. Primary documents read from raw EDGAR files:

- Netflix, Inc. final IPO prospectus, Form 424B4, filed 2002-05-23, prospectus dated 2002-05-22, accession `0001012870-02-002475`, raw file `investment/backtests/sources/NFLX-2002/0001012870-02-002475.txt`.

### Netflix IPO facts from the 424B4

- Business: Netflix offered a DVD subscription plan; standard plan was three titles out at once, no due dates, late fees or shipping charges, for `$19.95/month`; DVDs were selected on the website, shipped by first-class mail, and returned in prepaid mailers. Source: 424B4 text lines 90-92.
- Revenue / losses: FY2001 revenue `$75.9m`, net loss `$38.6m`; Q1 2002 revenue `$30.5m`, net loss `$4.5m`; accumulated deficit `$141.8m`. Source: 424B4 text lines 92-93 and lines 160-170 / 189-198.
- TAM stated by Netflix: in 2001 domestic consumers spent more than `$29bn` on in-home filmed entertainment; consumer video rentals and purchases were `$21bn`, or 71% of that market, according to Adams Media Research. Source: 424B4 text lines 94-96.
- DVD adoption: at end-2001 about `25m` U.S. households had standalone set-top DVD players, up 91% in 2001; Adams estimated `69m` households by 2006, or about 62% of U.S. TV households. Source: 424B4 text lines 97-100.
- Traction: Bay Area penetration was about `2.8%` of all households; more than `11,500` titles, with more than `11,000` selected by subscribers in April 2002; infrastructure processed `5.8m` DVDs/month. Source: 424B4 text lines 101-114.
- Shares outstanding after offering: `20,648,074` common shares, excluding options/warrants/ESPP. Source: 424B4 text lines 119-144.
- Potentially dilutive securities excluded from the share count: `4,352,472` options outstanding at weighted-average exercise price `$3.11`, `7,017,962` warrants at weighted-average exercise price `$3.20`, and `583,333` ESPP shares reserved. Source: 424B4 text lines 134-144.
- Fully-diluted share base used for script: `20,648,074 + 4,352,472 + 7,017,962 = 32,018,508` pre-split-adjustment shares. I exclude the ESPP reserve and ungranted option-pool availability from initial diluted shares and capture them in the dilution assumption. On the Yahoo split-adjusted price basis, shares = `32,018,508 × 140 = 4,482,591,120` shares. The command actually used `4,482,518,580`; this is a 0.0016% typo-level difference and does not affect the conclusion, but it is recorded here for audit.
- Net cash/debt after IPO: pro forma as adjusted cash `$76.471m`; long-term debt `$0.959m`; net cash about `$75.512m`. Source: 424B4 text lines 876-902. The valuation script input `net-debt` is terminal net debt, so I set terminal net debt to `0`, not `-75.5m`, because the IPO cash was growth runway rather than guaranteed 2010 excess cash.
- Use of proceeds: expected net proceeds about `$74.7m`; repay about `$14.1m` subordinated notes and use the rest for working capital/general corporate purposes. Source: 424B4 text lines 845-857.
- Competitive response risk: principal competitors included Blockbuster and Hollywood Entertainment; Blockbuster announced in April 2002 plans to test two subscription rental programs in summer 2002, including online subscription. Source: 424B4 text around lines 531-540 and raw grep line 1350.

### TAM projected to 2010, using pre-2002 expectations

Base TAM for `it-works` / `niche-survivor`: `$34.0bn` in 2010.

Basis: Hollywood Entertainment's FY2001 10-K cites Adams Media Research that the domestic video retail industry — rentals plus sales — grew from `$21.1bn` in 2000 to `$23.4bn` in 2001 and was expected to grow to over `$34.0bn` by 2010. Source: HLYW 2001 10-K lines 176-183. This is closest to Netflix's own `$21bn` 2001 rental-and-purchase market boundary.

Blue-sky TAM override: `$38.6bn` in 2010.

Basis: Blockbuster's FY2001 10-K cites Paul Kagan Associates that at-home movie consumer spending in the U.S. increased from about `$21.3bn` in 2000 to about `$22.2bn` in 2001 and was projected to rise to `$38.6bn` by 2010. Source: BBI 2001 10-K lines 193-205. I use this only for blue-sky because a category winner could plausibly expand beyond retail rental/sale into the broader at-home viewing wallet, but pre-2002 knowledge does not justify global streaming/media optionality.

### Role-model discipline and choices

#### It-works role model: Hollywood Entertainment (`HLYW`)

Why chosen: HLYW was the second-largest U.S. specialty retailer of rentable home videocassettes, DVDs and games, with about `1,800` stores and about `10%` share of the domestic videocassette/DVD rental market. Source: HLYW 2001 10-K lines 121-130. It is a real mid-scale success rather than the category giant. It had recovered from an overexpansion/e-commerce mistake and become cash-generative, making it a decent analogue for an established but not dominant survivor.

Inputs read from HLYW:

- Capture: `10%`, directly from HLYW's stated domestic rental-market share. Source: HLYW lines 121-130.
- Mature EBIT margin: `8.6%`, HLYW's 2001 income from operations as % of revenue. Source: HLYW lines 1410-1444.
- Exit multiple: `10x EBIT`. Weakest pre-2002 input: the raw 10-K gives margins but not a clean valuation multiple; I use a mature specialty-retailer EBIT multiple, deliberately not a high Internet multiple.
- Dilution: `5%/yr`; Netflix had a large option/warrant overhang and was still investing, but the IPO cash reduced near-term financing risk.

Strongest alternative rejected: Blockbuster. Rejected for it-works because it is the category giant, not a mid-scale success; using it for the base survivor case would inflate capture and brand economics.

#### Blue-sky role model: Blockbuster (`BBI`)

Why chosen: BBI was the world's leading rentable home videocassette/DVD/game provider with nearly `8,000` stores in the U.S., territories and 26 countries; brand recognition was near 100% among active U.S. movie renters. Source: BBI 2001 10-K lines 177-189 and 386-397. It is the natural real-world ceiling available in 2002.

Inputs read/derived from BBI:

- Capture: `20%` of the broader at-home movie spending boundary. BBI's worldwide revenue exceeded `$5.1bn`, 80% U.S.; U.S. revenue was therefore about `$4.1bn`, roughly 20% of the `$20.3bn` U.S. retail home video industry boundary. Source: BBI lines 187-200.
- Mature EBITDA margin: `11.6%`, computed as adjusted operating income excluding special items `$175.1m` plus depreciation `$246.6m` plus amortization `$177.1m`, divided by revenue `$5,156.7m`. Source: BBI lines 2240-2264 and lines 2371-2378.
- Exit multiple: `8x EBITDA`, lower than the it-works EBIT multiple because BBI was a mature low-growth retailer with litigation and format-transition risk.
- Dilution: `5%/yr`.

Strongest alternative rejected: Amazon. Rejected because in 2002 Amazon was a general e-commerce/logistics analogue, not a home-video rental/capture analogue; its economics would violate the same-market capture rule.

#### Niche-survivor role model: no clean public analogue; use Netflix-at-current-scale economics

No distinct public pure-play online DVD-rental survivor existed in May 2002. I therefore model niche-survivor as Netflix proving the subscription/mail concept but never escaping a small national niche.

Inputs:

- Capture: `1%` of the `$34bn` 2010 retail-home-video TAM.
- EBIT margin: `5%`, lower than HLYW because fixed fulfillment, postage, marketing and technology costs do not amortize well at sub-scale.
- Exit multiple: `8x EBIT`.
- Dilution: `10%/yr`, because weak survival would require more financing from a position of weakness.

Strongest alternative rejected: Reel.com / other online DVD sites. Rejected because Reel.com was shut down by Hollywood in June 2000 after cash losses, and the other online DVD rental sites named in Netflix's 424B4 were not public with usable mature economics. Source for Reel.com shutdown/cash-loss context: HLYW 2001 10-K lines 144-153 and 1724-1756.

### Scenario probabilities

Base band: Netflix is a listed commercial-traction company with scaling revenue but losses. The scenario-tam doc's starting band is failure `30-50%`, niche `25-40%`, it-works `10-25%`, blue-sky `2-5%`.

Chosen probabilities:

- Niche-survivor: `32%` — within band.
- It-works: `12%` — low end of band.
- Blue-sky: `3%` — within band.
- Failure: residual `53%` — slightly above the band, deliberately, because Blockbuster had already announced subscription tests and the home-video rental market was intensely competitive.

Stage-gate reasoning from 2002 facts:

- DVD adoption reaches broad enough installed base: high, because DVD households were projected from `25m` in 2001 to `69m` in 2006.
- Netflix nationalizes the Bay Area proof without breaking service quality: moderate; Bay Area penetration was `2.8%`, but national fulfillment and USPS dependency were unproven.
- Netflix survives incumbent response: low/moderate; Blockbuster and Hollywood had brand, stores, studio relationships and cash flow.
- Unit economics improve enough after marketing/postage/content costs: moderate; Q1 2002 adjusted EBITDA had turned slightly positive, but GAAP losses remained.

I do not silently force failure into the starting band because the incumbent-response gate is unusually live in May 2002.

### Dilution assumption

Initial share base already includes outstanding options and warrants. Future dilution is modeled at:

- `5%/yr` for it-works and blue-sky: the company is still early and stock-comp heavy, but IPO cash reduces financing pressure.
- `10%/yr` for niche-survivor: slower growth means raises happen at weaker prices.

The option/warrant pool was large: options `4.352m`, warrants `7.018m`, plus ESPP reserve `0.583m`, against `20.648m` post-offering shares. Source: 424B4 text lines 134-144.

### Cohort calibration — actual 2010 endings

Netflix 2002-era named competitors from its 424B4 included video rental outlets such as Blockbuster and Hollywood Entertainment; retailers Best Buy, Wal-Mart and Amazon.com; subscription entertainment services HBO/Showtime; pay-per-view/VOD; online DVD sites dvdovernight and Rentmydvd.com; Internet movie providers Movielink, Movies.com, CinemaNow and MovieFlix; cable providers AOL Time Warner and Comcast; and satellite providers DirecTV and Echostar. Source: Netflix 424B4 extracted text lines 531-559.

Observed outcomes by 2010:

- Netflix: blue-sky survivor, but via a route the Phase 1 model did not allow for — DVD-by-mail became a large national subscription business, then streaming became the strategic growth engine by 2010.
- Blockbuster / BBI role model: failure/distress. Blockbuster filed Chapter 11 on 2010-09-23; a 2011 SEC-filed DISH press release says Blockbuster and certain domestic subsidiaries filed voluntary Chapter 11 petitions on that date. This maps to the `failure` scenario for the incumbent giant, not the blue-sky role model ending.
- Hollywood Entertainment / HLYW role model: acquired by Movie Gallery in 2005, then the combined brick-and-mortar chain was liquidated in 2010. This maps to `failure`, despite being used as the Phase 1 it-works analogue.
- Wal-Mart online DVD rental: exited and promoted/migrated customers to Netflix in 2005. This maps to `failure/exit` for direct online DVD rental competition.
- Movielink: acquired by Blockbuster in 2007 and did not become an independent winner. This maps to `failure/absorbed`.
- CinemaNow: shifted through partnerships/acquisitions and by 2010 the brand rights moved to Best Buy/Sonic's digital-video offering; it did not become the standalone consumer subscription winner. This maps to `niche/absorbed`, not a Netflix-like outcome.
- Cable/satellite/HBO/retailers: survived as companies/categories, but did not stop Netflix from capturing a subscription-video beachhead by 2010. In the scenario set, their existence was an incumbent-response risk, not a direct analogue ending.
- Redbox: not a primary 2002 named competitor in the S-1 list, but by 2010 it was an important DVD-rental/kiosk competitor named in Netflix's 10-K. It validates the competitive-risk premise, but not the failure outcome.

Cohort calibration result: the scenario set got industry brutality right — most direct DVD-rental/online-video competitors failed or were absorbed — but it mapped that brutality onto Netflix too heavily. The actual path was an asymmetric one-winner outcome: Netflix survived while the obvious role models died.

## Phase 3 — Compare predicted vs realized

### Method-level result

- `tam-capture` locked prediction: `+17.73%/yr` in the it-works case; terminal price `0.4415`; return multiple `3.69x`.
- `scenario-tam` locked prediction: `-2.73%/yr` expected; expected terminal price `0.0959`; expected multiple `0.80x`; `85%` probability below entry.
- Realized: about `+36.62%/yr`; terminal price `1.4517`; return multiple `12.13x`.

Error:

- Tam-capture underpredicted by about `18.9 percentage points/yr` and `8.44x` multiple.
- Scenario-tam underpredicted by about `39.3 percentage points/yr` and `11.33x` multiple.

### Input-by-input error diagnosis

#### Entry price / initial market cap

Locked input: split-adjusted first-day close `0.119643`; initial diluted market cap about `$536m` using the split-adjusted diluted share base.

Diagnosis: entry price was not the error. The IPO was not obviously cheap on 2002 revenue/losses — about `7x` FY2001 revenue and `4.4x` run-rate Q1 2002 revenue on the diluted method used here. The return gap came from terminal economics and probabilities, not an entry-price data miss.

#### TAM

Locked input: `$34bn` 2010 retail home-video TAM; `$38.6bn` blue-sky broader at-home movie TAM.

Realized: Netflix revenue was `$2.16bn`, only `6.36%` of the `$34bn` denominator. The TAM denominator was not too small for an 8-year DVD/mail valuation. If anything, the base it-works TAM/capture combo was aggressive enough on market size. The miss was not TAM size.

However, the model failed to include a second curve: streaming/internet-delivered subscription video. By 2010 Netflix was already describing itself as primarily heading toward global streaming. That optionality did not show up in a one-period 2010 home-video TAM terminal model.

#### Capture / revenue

Locked tam-capture revenue: `$3.40bn` = 10% of `$34bn`.

Realized FY2010 revenue: `$2.16bn`.

Direction: the locked it-works capture/revenue was too high by about `$1.24bn`, or `57%` above realized revenue. This should have made the prediction too optimistic, not too conservative. So the ROI gap is not because we undershot 2010 revenue.

Flow-through: lower realized revenue would have reduced terminal value under the Phase 1 script if all else stayed equal.

#### Margin

Locked it-works margin: `8.6% EBIT`, from HLYW.

Realized margin: `13.1% EBIT`.

Direction: margin was too low by `4.5 percentage points`, or about `52%` relative to the locked margin.

Flow-through: replacing `8.6%` with `13.1%` on the locked `$3.40bn` revenue would raise EBIT from `$292m` to `$445m`, increasing terminal equity by roughly `52%` before dilution. But this alone cannot explain a `12.1x` realized stock multiple versus the `3.7x` tam-capture forecast.

Interpretation: using store-chain margins understated the scalability of a national subscription platform with software-driven personalization and, by 2010, streaming leverage. HLYW was the wrong economics analogue even though it was a disciplined same-market analogue.

#### Exit multiple

Locked it-works multiple: `10x EBIT`.

Realized market-implied multiple: at 2010-05-24 split-adjusted close `1.451714`, adjusted shares comparable to Yahoo's post-split basis are roughly `54.304m × 140 = 7.60256bn`; implied market cap about `$11.0bn`. Against FY2010 EBIT `$283.641m`, that is about `38.9x EBIT`. Against FY2010 revenue, about `5.1x sales`.

Direction: exit multiple was far too low.

Flow-through: this is the single largest tam-capture error. Applying roughly `39x` EBIT rather than `10x` EBIT is a ~3.9x terminal-value uplift. The market was already valuing Netflix as an Internet/media growth platform, not a mature specialty retailer.

#### Dilution / shares

Locked input: initial diluted share base included options and warrants; future dilution `5%/yr`, giving `+47.7%` total shares.

Realized: initial pre-split diluted base was `~32.0m`; FY2010 diluted weighted-average shares were `54.304m`, a `+69.6%` increase. On the Yahoo split-adjusted basis, FY2010 shares would be about `7.60bn` versus locked exit shares `6.62bn`.

Direction: future dilution was too low by about `15%` relative to locked exit shares, or `22 percentage points` of total dilution from the 2002 base.

Flow-through: this error reduced actual per-share value relative to the script; it made the forecast too optimistic. It does not explain the underprediction.

#### Scenario probabilities

Locked probabilities: failure `53%`, niche `32%`, it-works `12%`, blue-sky `3%`.

Realized cohort: Netflix landed in the blue-sky branch by stock return and strategic positioning, while many apparent incumbents/analogues failed. The blue-sky probability was too low, and the model treated incumbent strength as mainly a Netflix failure risk rather than also as evidence that a pure-play disruptor could be unusually valuable if it crossed the survival chasm.

Direction: the `scenario-tam` miss is mostly probability/optionality, not base-case arithmetic. The model's `85%` below-entry probability was directionally wrong because the payoff distribution was more asymmetric than specified.

#### Role models

HLYW and BBI were the right *source discipline* for 2002: same customer wallet, public filings, scaled real economics. In hindsight they were the wrong *economic kind* of analogue:

- They were inventory/location retail chains, not software-mediated subscription networks.
- Their moat was store density and brand; Netflix's emerging moat became recommendation data, queue/subscription habit, national fulfillment, and then device/platform distribution.
- Their decline was not just a risk to Netflix; it became part of Netflix's opportunity.

The most revealing hindsight fact is that both chosen role models ended in distress/absorption while Netflix became the winner. A method that forces role models to be incumbents can miss disruptive entrants when the incumbent economics are precisely what gets disrupted.

### Why tam-capture did better than scenario-tam

Tam-capture asked: if Netflix works, what might the equity earn? It still understated the actual return, but it at least pointed positive at `+17.7%/yr`.

Scenario-tam asked: how likely is each branch? It over-penalized the survival risk and under-modeled the right tail. That made it more realistic-looking but much more wrong for a rare category winner.

The uncomfortable lesson: scenario-tam can become falsely conservative when the analyst recognizes many ways to die but fails to price the ways survival changes the market's valuation frame.

## Method lessons and proposed doc/script improvements

Do not edit the skills docs/scripts automatically; these are proposals for review only.

1. Add a `disruptor analogue` role-model slot when the target is explicitly attacking incumbent distribution. The model should still use same-market incumbents, but separately ask whether incumbent margins/multiples are the ceiling or the thing being disrupted.

2. Split terminal value into `core TAM value` and `optionality/platform value`. For Netflix 2002, the core DVD-by-mail TAM was enough to survive, but the realized multiple reflected streaming/platform optionality that existed as a risk/possibility in 2002 even if it was not forecastable in detail.

3. Add a script input for `terminal multiple scenario` or `multiple expansion branch`. Current tam-capture makes revenue/capture/margin explicit but leaves the multiple as a single fragile analyst pick. Here, multiple error dominated.

4. In scenario-tam, add a calibration prompt: “If the company survives, do incumbent failures increase the survivor's capture/multiple?” The current framing treated incumbent response mostly as failure probability, missing the winner-take-most aftermath.

5. Add explicit `actual realized input replay` mode to the scripts: entry price + realized revenue/margin/shares/multiple should decompose the gap into revenue, margin, multiple and dilution effects mechanically.

6. Add a warning when the role model's business architecture differs materially even if TAM is the same. HLYW and BBI were same-wallet analogues, but their store-heavy economics were structurally different from Netflix's subscription/software model.

7. Add anti-contamination ergonomics for backtests: a script wrapper that creates a Phase 1 lock file and refuses post-date data sources until the file exists. This exercise had a disclosed accidental Yahoo leakage event; tooling should make that harder.

8. For early disruptors, allow blue-sky probability to remain low but make blue-sky payoff structurally larger than “giant incumbent economics.” Here the right tail was not merely becoming Blockbuster online; it was becoming a different category.
