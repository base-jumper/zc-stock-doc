# Trait: Conservative Debt / Financial Strength

**What we're looking for:**
A balance sheet that the business could carry through a full cycle **without the debt ever forcing
its hand** — leverage low enough, coverage fat enough, and maturities far enough out that a downturn
is something the company rides through (or exploits) rather than something its creditors resolve for
it. Scored as resilience: **high = conservatively financed**. This is Buffett's *"good returns while
employing little or no debt"* criterion, and it is here because the whole wonderful-and-fair thesis is
*compounding you can underwrite for years* — and debt service is the claim that sits ahead of
reinvestment in the good years and breaks the compounding chain in the bad ones.

**This is a graded resilience read, not a solvency veto and not a quality signal.** It owns the
middle ground:

* The **catastrophic** end — imminent covenant breach, a maturity that forces a dilutive raise, cash
  burn that wipes out equity — is a binary kill handled by the [disqualifier gate](../disqualifiers.md)
  (solvency is a universal disqualifier). This trait grades the *prudent-to-aggressive* spectrum
  *before* things get that bad.
* **Leverage flattering ROE** — debt manufacturing a high *return on equity* — is the
  [returns-on-capital](returns-on-capital.md) trait's distortion check, about not being *fooled*. This
  trait is about the *risk the debt creates*, a separate question.

**Why this matters:**
A wonderful business carries a temporary earnings dip as a non-event, or an opportunity — it keeps
reinvesting, buys its own stock cheaply, and takes share. Add debt and the same dip becomes a forced
event: interest and maturities don't fall with earnings, so cash that should compound at the
business's [return on capital](returns-on-capital.md) is diverted to creditors, and a covenant or a
refinancing can compel a dividend cut, a fire-sale, or an equity raise at a depressed price exactly
when the business should be on offense. That is how a *recoverable* dip becomes a *permanent*
impairment of per-share value — and over a multi-year hold you are near-certain to live through at
least one such test.

**The test is appropriateness, not the absolute number.** The right level of debt is set by the
**stability of the cash flows servicing it**. Contractual, regulated, or recurring cash flows safely
support far more leverage than volatile, cyclical ones — which is why Buffett tolerates substantial,
non-recourse debt at regulated utilities and railroads but wants *cyclicals* run with almost none.
Read the leverage against the business type, not against a single universal line. 

**This trait scores the debt, not the volatility.** Cash-flow lumpiness only sets *which band* the
leverage is judged against — it is not itself penalized here. A cyclical business carrying little or no
debt therefore scores **high** on this trait: its lumpiness is [earnings-quality](earnings-quality.md)'s
concern.

**What to look at (easily obtainable metrics):**

* **Leverage — net debt / EBITDA.** Net debt (total debt − cash) over EBITDA. The headline gearing
  number; judge it against the business type below.
* **Interest coverage — EBIT / net interest** (EBITDA / interest as a cross-check). How many times
  over operating earnings cover the interest bill — the cushion before debt service bites.
* **Refinancing risk — debt maturities vs FCF and liquidity.** The maturity ladder (laddered vs a
  concentrated "wall"), near-term maturities (next 1–2 years) against cash on hand + committed
  facilities + a year or two of free cash flow, and the fixed/floating split. The question is whether
  the company *must* tap potentially-closed credit markets to survive, or can self-fund and refinance
  at leisure.

**Putting numbers on it — net debt / EBITDA by business type:**
Use these as **anchors, not a mechanical rule**; the spread to the next column widens or narrows with
how stable the cash flows are. A through-cycle figure beats a peak-of-cycle one — a cyclical at 2× at
the top can be 4×+ in the trough on the same debt.

| Business type (cash-flow stability) | Conservative (0.70–1.00) | Stretched (0.40–0.69) | Aggressive / dangerous (0.00–0.39) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Regulated utility / contracted infrastructure** (stable, contractual) | ≤ ~4× | ~4–5× | > ~5–6× |
| **Stable operating co.** (staples, healthcare, recurring/software-like) | net cash to ~2× | ~2–3× | > ~3× |
| **Cyclical** (industrial, materials, autos, semis, energy) | net cash to ~1× *through cycle* | ~1–2.5× | > ~2.5–3× (trough multiplies it) |
| **Capital-light compounder** (brands, asset-light services) | net cash — ideal | up to ~1× | any meaningful debt is a yellow flag *for the type* |
| **Banks / insurers / financials** | n/a — leverage *is* the business | — | judge on regulatory capital ratios; defer the solvency extreme to the [disqualifier gate](../disqualifiers.md) |

**Interest coverage (EBIT / net interest):**

| Reading | Coverage | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **Comfortable** (0.70–1.00) | ≥ ~6–8× | a stable utility is safe lower; a cyclical needs *more* cushion because EBIT falls in the downturn |
| **Adequate** (0.40–0.69) | ~3–6× | real but unremarkable headroom |
| **Thin / stressed** (0.00–0.39) | < ~3× | little margin before a dip threatens debt service |

**Key questions:**

* What is net debt / EBITDA, and is it conservative *for this type of business* and its cash-flow
  stability — measured through the cycle, not at the peak?
* How many times does EBIT cover net interest, and how much would coverage fall in a realistic
  downturn?
* Are the next 1–2 years of maturities covered by cash, committed facilities, and free cash flow — or
  is there a wall that depends on open credit markets?
* Is the leverage *matched* to the cash flows servicing it (stable/contractual) or *mismatched*
  (volatile/cyclical debt run hot)?
* Are there off-balance-sheet or hidden claims — operating leases, pension deficits, factoring — that
  understate true leverage? (If serious enough to threaten solvency, that is a
  [disqualifier](../disqualifiers.md).)

**Scoring guidance:**

* **0.70–1.00** Conservatively financed for its type: low leverage (often net cash for capital-light
  businesses), comfortable interest coverage, and well-laddered maturities self-funded from cash and
  FCF. A downturn is something the business rides through or exploits.
* **0.40–0.69** Stretched but not endangered: leverage toward the upper end for its type, adequate but
  unremarkable coverage, or some near-term maturities reliant on refinancing. Debt would constrain the
  business in a downturn — divert cash from reinvestment, pressure the payout — without threatening
  survival.
* **0.00–0.39** Aggressively financed: high leverage for its type, thin coverage, or a maturity wall
  dependent on open credit markets — debt that a downturn could turn into forced selling, dilution, or
  worse. (Where this crosses into imminent solvency risk, it also trips a
  [disqualifier](../disqualifiers.md).)

**Documentation:**

* Net debt / EBITDA, with the source figures (net debt, EBITDA) and the business type it is judged
  against — and a through-cycle read where the business is cyclical
* Interest coverage (EBIT / net interest), and a sense of how far it would fall in a realistic downturn
* The maturity profile: near-term maturities vs cash + facilities + FCF, whether laddered or a wall,
  and the fixed/floating split
* Whether the leverage is matched to the stability of the cash flows servicing it (the
  [earnings-quality](earnings-quality.md) verdict), with the reasoning
* Any off-balance-sheet or hidden claims considered
* The reason for the score — why the balance sheet is judged resilient (or not) for *this* business
  through a full cycle

## Script

[`conservative_debt.py`](../../scripts/conservative_debt.py) gathers the metrics above from Yahoo (via
[`yfin`](../../../yahoo-finance/SKILL.md)) and computes the ratios, so you score off prepared numbers
rather than pulling statements by hand:

```bash
conservative_debt CBA.AX            # 5 annual years (default); --years N to widen
conservative_debt CBA.AX --format json
```

It answers the trait's three pillars — leverage, coverage, refinancing risk — in two views:

* a **current snapshot** that doesn't lag the last filing: net debt / EBITDA from `info` plus interest
  coverage computed from the TTM income statement (EBIT and EBITDA over net interest), with the
  liquidity ratios; and
* an **annual series** (newest first) carrying, per year, net debt / EBITDA, EBIT interest coverage,
  and a **self-funding ratio** — `(cash + a year's FCF) / current debt`, the times a year's maturities
  are covered without tapping credit markets (the refinancing-risk read) — alongside the debt split.
  Net debt / FCF, EBITDA coverage, debt / equity and capitalised leases ride along in `--format json`.

A through-cycle summary (peak leverage, weakest coverage, latest self-funding, leverage trend) makes
the cyclical read one glance. Annual figures are the authoritative leverage read; the snapshot's
`info`-derived total debt runs higher because Yahoo folds leases into it, so use it only as a recency
cross-check.

The script stops at the facts: it **does not score and does not assign a band** — which column of the
tables above a figure lands in depends on the business type and a through-cycle read, which is your
call. It surfaces the sector/industry to inform it, and lists what Yahoo can't see (the maturity
ladder, fixed/floating split, undrawn facilities, and off-balance-sheet claims) so those go to
primary sources. Banks/financials come back sparse by design — there is no conventional leverage to
compute; judge them on regulatory capital per the table above.
