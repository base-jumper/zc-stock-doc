# NVDA 1999 back-test — Phase 1 locked prediction

**Lock status:** Phase 1 written before looking up any post-January-1999 realized outcomes.  
**As-of date pretended:** 1999-01-22, NVIDIA IPO/first trading day.  
**Method:** raw-mode `tam_capture_valuation.py` and `scenario_tam_valuation.py`; no stock-doc was created or modified.

## Guardrail log

I used only documents/data dated before February 1999 for Phase-1 inputs, except the Yahoo historical quote for **1999-01-22 itself**, because the prompt specified the first-day close from Yahoo. I did **not** look up NVIDIA's later prices, later financials, or the later fates of 3dfx/S3/ATI before this section was written.

## Primary contemporaneous sources

- NVIDIA final IPO prospectus / 424B4, filed 1999-01-22, accession `0001012870-99-000192`.
- NVIDIA Yahoo historical OHLC record for 1999-01-22 only.
- S3 Inc. Form 10-Q for quarter ended 1998-09-30, filed 1998-11-16, accession `0000891618-98-004942`.
- 3Dfx Interactive Form 10-Q for quarter ended 1998-09-30, filed 1998-11-16, accession `0001010026-98-000008`.
- Intel Form 10-Q for quarter ended 1998-09-26, filed 1998-11-10, accession `0000050863-98-000099`.
- IDC PC-shipment forecast article dated 1998 (EE Times), citing IDC's 1998 worldwide PC shipment forecast of 90.6m units and 1997 shipments of 79.9m.

## Prospectus facts used

### Business/market setup

NVIDIA described itself as designing, developing and marketing 3D graphics processors and related software for the mainstream PC market. The S-1 positioned 3D graphics as emerging, game-led, and increasingly necessary for mainstream PCs, but still dependent on broader software/OEM adoption.

Key prospectus market quote:

> Mercury Research estimates that 3D graphics will be standard in every PC unit shipped by 2001. Mercury Research also estimates that 4.9 million 3D graphics processors were sold worldwide in 1997 and 138 million will be sold worldwide in 2001.

This is the core TAM starting point. The prospectus gave unit volume, not dollar TAM.

### Share count and net cash/debt at IPO

- Common shares offered: **3.5m**.
- Common stock outstanding after offering: **28,595,976**.
- Prospectus excluded **8,662,120** option shares at a $4.82 weighted-average exercise price and **158,806** warrants at a $2.10 weighted-average exercise price.
- Fully diluted entry share count used for valuation: **37,416,902** pre-split equivalent = `28,595,976 + 8,662,120 + 158,806`.
- Yahoo split-adjusted 1999-01-22 close: **$0.037559062**. To keep this consistent with prospectus shares, I multiplied shares by the same 480x split factor implied by Yahoo's adjusted series: **17,960,112,960** split-adjusted shares. This preserves the same entry market cap as using unadjusted price and unadjusted shares.
- Entry market cap from script: **$674.57m**.
- Cash and cash equivalents, pro forma as adjusted: **$49.721m**.
- Capital lease obligations: **$3.875m** total present value, including $1.843m current and $2.032m long-term.
- Bank line: **$5.0m outstanding** as of 1998-10-25; prospectus said ~$5.0m of IPO proceeds may be used to repay it.
- IPO net cash estimate: **~$40.8m** after cash less bank line and capital lease obligations. I did **not** feed IPO net cash into terminal `net-debt`; the scripts' `net-debt` field is terminal net debt, and I set terminal net debt to zero because the 8-year mature case was not underwritten as debt-financed.

## TAM build, as of January 1999

The prospectus/Mercury source gave 3D graphics processor units growing from **4.9m in 1997** to **138m in 2001**, with 3D standard in every PC by 2001. For an 8-year horizon to 2007, the narrow base TAM is therefore no longer “3D adoption” but “PC graphics processors shipped with PCs.”

Inputs used:

- 2001 base: **138m units** from Mercury via NVIDIA prospectus.
- 2001→2007 PC-unit growth: **10%/yr estimate**, anchored to pre-1999 IDC data showing worldwide PC units grew 15.2% in 1997 to 79.9m and were forecast to grow 13.4% in 1998 to 90.6m, then tapered to avoid extrapolating the explosive 1997→2001 3D-adoption CAGR beyond saturation.
- 2007 processor units: **~244m**.
- Graphics processor ASP: **$20 estimate**, flagged because I found no pre-Feb-1999 prospectus line giving industry ASP. NVIDIA warned ASPs would decline as volumes rose, so this is intentionally below high-end add-in-board economics and meant to approximate chip revenue, not full board/system value.
- Base 2007 TAM: **$4.9bn** (`~244m units × $20`).

### Blue-sky TAM override

For blue-sky I allowed **$8.0bn** instead of $4.9bn. The January-1999 argument is that the same 3D graphics architecture could plausibly reach beyond mainstream PC attach into higher-performance PC/workstation graphics and game/consumer 3D acceleration, all categories named in the prospectus's competitive landscape. This is still a graphics-processor boundary, not a modern AI/data-center boundary; I deliberately did **not** include markets that were not visible from the 1999 prospectus.

## Role-model anchors available by January 1999

### S3 / graphics niche-survivor anchor

S3 was an established graphics-chip supplier named in the NVIDIA prospectus. Its 1998 Q3 10-Q showed:

- Nine-month 1998 net sales: **$183.092m**.
- Nine-month 1998 operating loss: **$(93.139)m**.
- Nine-month 1997 net sales: **$334.448m**.
- Nine-month 1997 operating income: **$8.808m**, a **2.6% operating margin**.

I used S3 as the niche-survivor role model because it was a real, established graphics player showing how brutally sub-scale economics could look during the 3D transition.

### ATI/S3 it-works anchor

ATI was named by NVIDIA as an internally-developed graphics add-in-board competitor. I did not find a clean pre-Feb-1999 EDGAR filing for ATI because ATI was Canadian; therefore the “ATI/S3” it-works anchor is a contemporaneous estimate rather than a fully sourced filing read. I used established-graphics economics bounded by S3's historical positive margin and NVIDIA/3Dfx's current gross-margin evidence:

- Capture: **16%** of the $4.9bn 2007 base graphics-processor TAM.
- EBIT margin: **10%**.
- Exit multiple: **18x EBIT**.

This is meant to represent “successful established player” rather than category monopoly.

### Intel blue-sky ceiling

Intel was the natural mature semiconductor ceiling and was also an explicit new graphics competitor via i740 in the NVIDIA prospectus. Intel's 1998 Q3 10-Q showed:

- Q3 1998 revenue: **$6.731bn**.
- Q3 1998 operating income: **$2.156bn**, or **~32% operating margin**.
- First-nine-month 1998 revenue: **$18.659bn**.
- First-nine-month 1998 operating income: **$5.543bn**, or **~30% operating margin**.
- Intel stated that microprocessors based on P6 architecture represented a majority of revenue and substantial majority of gross margin.

For blue-sky I used Intel-like EBIT margin/multiple economics, but **not** Intel's whole CPU TAM; the scenario TAM override is the expanded graphics-processor boundary visible from 1999.

## Dilution

The S-1 already showed substantial latent dilution: **8.662m options** plus **0.159m warrants** on top of 28.596m post-offering shares, or ~31% extra before future grants. It also reserved **2.592m** future equity-plan shares, **0.206m** director-plan shares, and **0.500m** ESPP shares. On a fully diluted base, that additional pool is ~8.8% pre-split.

Scenario dilution inputs:

- Niche-survivor: **8%/yr**, reflecting continued weakness and the prospectus's capital needs.
- It-works: **5%/yr**, reflecting already-heavy option overhang plus further employee equity but less distress issuance.
- Blue-sky: **4%/yr**, reflecting faster success and less financing pressure.

## Probability reasoning

NVIDIA was not pre-revenue: revenue had scaled from **$29.071m in 1997** to **$92.700m for the nine months ended 1998**, but it was still loss-making and exposed to extreme product-cycle, manufacturing-yield, customer concentration, patent-litigation and competition risk. I therefore started from the `scenario-tam.md` **commercial traction** band:

- failure: 30–50%
- niche-survivor: 25–40%
- it-works: 10–25%
- blue-sky: 2–5%

Stage-gate read from 1999 facts:

- 3D adoption gate: favorable — Mercury expected 3D in every PC by 2001.
- NVIDIA product/design-win gate: favorable but not locked — products were in six of top ten PC OEMs' products and had major add-in-board customers, but design wins reset every spring/fall cycle.
- Competition gate: harsh — Intel i740, ATI, S3, Matrox, Trident, 3Dlabs/SGI/Intergraph, 3Dfx and VideoLogic were all explicitly named competitors.
- Manufacturing/customer gate: negative — TSMC yield issues, STB/3Dfx consolidation risk, and customer concentration were major S-1 risks.
- Legal/IP gate: negative — SGI, S3 and 3Dfx patent suits were pending.

Chosen probabilities:

- Failure: residual **47%**.
- Niche-survivor: **35%**.
- It-works: **15%**.
- Blue-sky: **3%**.

This stays within the commercial-traction bands and intentionally leaves most probability mass below a clean success outcome.

## Warning responses

The TAM-capture run produced no warning lines.

Scenario-TAM warnings copied verbatim and answered:

> WARNING: implied tail alpha 0.69 outside 1.5-3.0: fatter tail than venture history supports (alpha ~ 2)

Answer: I accept the warning. The blue-sky terminal value is much larger than the it-works terminal value while only 5x less probable (3% vs 15%). A stricter power-law discipline would either cut blue-sky probability materially or reduce the blue-sky terminal build. I left it unchanged because, from a January-1999 outside view, NVIDIA had real design-win traction in a market Mercury expected to become universal across PCs; however this is clearly the most aggressive assumption in Phase 1.

> WARNING: blue-sky (3% probable) contributes 70% of the expected value — the estimate is tail-dominated; the probability deserves extra scrutiny

Answer: I accept this warning too. The expected return is being carried by a low-probability Intel-like outcome, not by the base it-works case. That means the scenario-TAM prediction should be read less as “NVIDIA is probably cheap” and more as “the only reason this clears at all is the small chance of category dominance.” The warning is especially important because the market-implied breakeven output says even the it-works case alone cannot justify the entry price at a 10% hurdle.

## Locked Phase-1 prediction

- TAM-capture “it works” ROI: **+4.44%/yr**, **1.42x** over 8 years.
- Scenario-TAM expected ROI: **-1.45%/yr**, **0.89x** expected multiple over 8 years.
- Probability of ending below entry: **82.0%**.
- Failure probability: **47.0%** residual.
- Prediction interpretation: at the January-1999 first-day close, the method says the IPO price already capitalized a large amount of success. The narrow it-works case is positive but below a 10% hurdle, and the probability-weighted case is slightly negative unless one gives materially more probability/value to the blue-sky outcome.

# Phase 2 — Realized outcomes, gathered after Phase-1 lock

## NVIDIA realized 8-year outcome

**Price source:** Yahoo split-adjusted historical OHLC, checked only after Phase 1 was written.

- Entry close, 1999-01-22: **$0.037559062**.
- January 2007 close used for the 8-year mark: **2007-01-31 close $0.467778653**.
- Realized multiple: **12.45x**.
- Realized annualized ROI: **+37.06%/yr**.

**2007 10-K source:** NVIDIA Form 10-K filed 2007-03-16, accession `0001045810-07-000008`, fiscal year ended 2007-01-28.

Realized model inputs:

- Revenue: **$3.069bn**.
- Operating income / EBIT: **$453.452m**.
- EBIT margin: **14.78%**.
- Net income: **$448.834m**; net margin **14.63%**.
- Diluted shares: **391.504m**, as reported after NVIDIA's April 2006 2-for-1 split. To compare against Yahoo's fully split-adjusted price series, this is **23.490bn** shares after the later 60x split adjustment.
- Realized terminal equity value implied by Yahoo adjusted price and adjusted diluted shares: **~$10.99bn**.
- Realized dilution from the split-adjusted IPO share base: **+30.8% total**, versus the locked it-works assumption of **+47.7% total**.
- Market/share evidence:
  - NVIDIA reported GPU Business revenue of **$1.994bn** and consolidated revenue of **$3.069bn**.
  - It reported growing its performance-segment share from **79% to 85%** per Mercury Research Q4 2005/2006 PC Graphics Reports.
  - It was the largest AMD64 chipset supplier with **53% segment share**.
  - It reported leadership in desktop standalone GPU throughout fiscal 2007 and **58% notebook GPU share** in Q4 fiscal 2007.
  - The 10-K does not give a single all-graphics-market share number. On the Phase-1 base-TAM denominator, consolidated revenue was **62.6%** of the $4.9bn TAM and GPU Business revenue was **40.7%** of it; those are not clean market-share numbers because the realized revenue mix included MCP/chipset, handheld, consumer-electronics and memory revenue.

## Cohort endings for scenario calibration

### 3dfx — failure / asset-sale liquidation

3dfx's 2002 Form 10-K states that shareholders approved liquidation and the sale of substantially all assets to NVIDIA. NVIDIA paid **$70.0m cash** and potentially **2.0m NVIDIA shares** only if 3dfx could certify that debts/liabilities were provided for or could be satisfied below a threshold. 3dfx's later liquidation filings showed **net liabilities in liquidation** rather than residual net assets; NVIDIA's 2007 10-K also states that if 3dfx could not satisfy the certification conditions, NVIDIA had no obligation to pay additional consideration.

**Shareholder recovery conclusion:** common shareholders effectively recovered **zero / near-zero**. This maps to **failure**, not niche-survivor.

### S3 → SONICblue — niche decline became failure

S3 exited the graphics-chip path into SONICblue/consumer electronics. SONICblue's September 2002 10-Q still showed positive stockholders' equity of only **$7.398m**, then the company filed Chapter 11 on 2003-03-21 and sought to sell ReplayTV/Rio/GoVideo assets. Later monthly operating reports showed total assets around **$79–82m** but equity deficits around **$(158)m**.

**Terminal-value conclusion:** the common equity was effectively wiped out. Operationally this looked like “niche decline,” but financially it maps to **failure** by terminal shareholder outcome.

### ATI — successful established-player outcome

ATI was acquired by AMD in 2006. AMD's July 2006 merger 8-K valued the deal at **$20.47 per ATI share**, consisting of **$16.40 cash + 0.2229 AMD shares**, or about **$5.4bn** based on AMD's 2006-07-21 close. AMD's October 2006 completion 8-K states final consideration of **$21.36 per ATI share**, **$4.3bn cash + 57.946m AMD shares**.

**Shareholder outcome conclusion:** ATI holders received a real strategic-exit payoff, not a wipeout. This maps to **it-works / established-player success**, below NVIDIA's realized blue-sky but far above the failure/niche outcomes.

# Phase 3 — Predicted vs realized

## Headline comparison

| Measure | Locked prediction | Realized | Error |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| TAM-capture “it works” ROI | **+4.44%/yr** | **+37.06%/yr** | **-32.62 pp/yr** |
| TAM-capture multiple | **1.42x** | **12.45x** | **8.8x too low** |
| Scenario-TAM expected ROI | **-1.45%/yr** | **+37.06%/yr** | **-38.52 pp/yr** |
| Scenario-TAM expected multiple | **0.89x** | **12.45x** | **14.0x too low** |
| Scenario-TAM P(below entry) | **82%** | No | Wrong side of distribution |

The locked scenario set did contain a blue-sky case with **+46.1%/yr** ROI, so NVIDIA's realized +37.1%/yr was not outside the scenario universe. The mistake was probability/central-case placement: the realized outcome landed between the locked it-works and blue-sky cases, much closer to blue-sky than the 3% probability implied.

## Input-by-input comparison

| Input | Locked it-works assumption | Realized / observed | Direction of error |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Revenue / TAM capture | $784m revenue = 16% of $4.9bn TAM | $3.069bn consolidated revenue; $1.994bn GPU revenue | Much too low |
| EBIT margin | 10% | 14.78% | Too low |
| Exit multiple | 18x EBIT | Implied ~24.2x EBIT from terminal equity value / EBIT | Too low |
| Dilution | +47.7% total over 8y | +30.8% total | Too punitive |
| Terminal equity value | $1.41bn | ~$10.99bn | Too low by ~7.8x |
| Scenario probability | Blue-sky 3%; below-entry 82% | Outcome was near blue-sky | Blue-sky probability too low |

A mechanical bridge from the locked it-works terminal price to realized price shows the error stack:

- Locked it-works terminal price: **$0.0532**, **1.42x**, **+4.44%/yr**.
- Substitute actual consolidated revenue, but keep predicted 10% margin / 18x multiple / 5% dilution: **$0.2082**, **5.54x**, **+23.87%/yr**.
- Substitute actual revenue and actual EBIT margin, keep 18x multiple / predicted dilution: **$0.3076**, **8.19x**, **+30.06%/yr**.
- Substitute actual terminal market value but keep predicted dilution: **$0.4141**, **11.03x**, **+34.99%/yr**.
- Actual all-in terminal price: **$0.4678**, **12.45x**, **+37.06%/yr**.

So the biggest miss was not the exit multiple or dilution. It was **revenue/TAM boundary/capture**: NVIDIA became more than a 16%-share graphics-chip participant. It became a high-share GPU leader and expanded into adjacent chipset/consumer/professional/notebook categories already faintly visible in 1999, then by late 2006 had CUDA/programmable-GPU optionality beginning to emerge.

## What the method got right

- The scenario framework was directionally useful: it explicitly preserved a blue-sky path, and that path was the only one large enough to resemble the realized result.
- The high failure probability was justified by cohort evidence. 3dfx and S3/SONICblue prove that the 1999 competitive risk was not theoretical.
- The script warnings were valuable. The file warned that expected value was tail-dominated. History says that was exactly the crux: the investment depended on whether NVIDIA deserved more blue-sky probability than a generic commercial-traction company.

## What the method got wrong

- **TAM boundary was too narrow.** The base TAM was built as graphics-processor chip revenue. By 2007 NVIDIA's revenue included GPUs plus MCP/chipsets, professional/workstation, notebook, handheld, consumer-electronics and memory. Some of these were visible in 1999 as adjacent graphics/consumer/workstation markets, but the model treated them mostly as blue-sky rather than a serious expansion path.
- **Capture definition was too conservative for category leaders.** The docs say role-model capture should be share of the role model's own market. For a fabless specialist in a consolidating performance market, a 16% “it works” capture missed the possibility of dominant standalone/performance share.
- **Blue-sky probability was too low.** Given NVIDIA's pre-IPO revenue ramp, OEM design wins, named top-ten-PC-OEM presence, and Mercury's universal-3D adoption forecast, 3% was probably too stingy. The warning made this visible but did not force recalibration.
- **Failure/niche separation was too soft.** S3/SONICblue shows a company can look like “niche survivor” operationally for a while but still be a common-equity failure. The scenario set should distinguish “survives as a product/company” from “common shareholders earn a terminal return.”

## Proposed doc/script improvements for review

1. **Add a “TAM boundary audit” checklist to scenario-TAM docs.** Require explicit base, adjacent-visible, and non-visible TAM layers. In 1999 NVIDIA, base PC graphics was valid, but workstation/notebook/chipset/consumer graphics deserved a separately probability-weighted adjacent-visible layer.
2. **Add optional scenario-level revenue components.** Instead of one TAM × capture per scenario, allow multiple TAM legs, e.g. `pc_gpu`, `workstation`, `chipset`, `consumer`, each with separate capture/margin assumptions.
3. **Add a leadership-stage gate.** If a company has fast revenue scale, top-tier OEM design wins, and a market forecast to become universal, the script/docs should prompt a blue-sky probability sanity check against 3–5% defaults.
4. **Add cohort-base-rate prompts.** Require at least three named cohort outcomes: zero/wipeout, mediocre survival, and strategic winner. This back-test shows why: 3dfx/S3 support the failure bucket, while ATI supports it-works and NVIDIA shows the rare leader tail.
5. **Separate operational outcome from shareholder outcome.** A “niche survivor” that dilutes, pivots, or files Chapter 11 should score as shareholder failure. The docs should make terminal common-equity recovery the deciding criterion.
6. **Add a warning for tail probability vs evidence.** The existing tail-dominance warning is good; add a follow-up prompt: “What concrete as-of-date evidence would justify increasing blue-sky probability?” That would have forced a clearer debate around NVIDIA's OEM/design-win evidence.

## Back-test conclusion

The method did not miss because it ignored failure risk; failure risk was real. It missed because it underweighted the rare category-leader path and bounded TAM too narrowly. For 1999 NVIDIA, a disciplined investor needed to ask: “Is this just another graphics-chip company, or the likely consolidating leader of programmable visual computing?” The locked model answered mostly the former, with only 3% on the latter. History punished that conservatism.
