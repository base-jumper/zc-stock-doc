# Sleep Apnea And CPAP Therapy

## Market Definition

The benchmark market covers positive airway pressure therapy for obstructive sleep apnea: CPAP, APAP,
and bilevel flow generators; patient interfaces; tubing; humidification; filters; replacement masks
and accessories; and adherence software bundled with therapy. It excludes sleep diagnostics, sleep-lab
services, home sleep testing, oral appliances, implantable neurostimulation, surgery, oxygen therapy
sold outside PAP therapy, and hospital acute-care ventilation.

The 10-year benchmark year is 2005. PAP therapy had become a large, fast-growing reimbursed OSA
device market by then, with recurring masks and accessories already important, but it was still
earlier than the later 2020 mature installed-base and cloud-adherence market.

## Current View

The base-year TAM estimate of `$0.18B` is an approximate 1995 manufacturer-revenue pool. The direct
historical anchors are ResMed's FY1995 revenue of `$23.5M`, Respironics' FY1995 total net sales of
`$99.45M`, and the later Respironics disclosure that OSA products represented `$98.9M` of FY1997
revenue. ResMed also disclosed that more than 100,000 new US patients were prescribed CPAP in 1995,
with flow generators sold to end users at roughly `$1,000-$3,000` and masks replaced every 12-18
months at roughly `$100-$200`.

The 10-year TAM estimate of `$1.0B` uses Fisher & Paykel Healthcare's FY2005 annual report, which
described OSA as a roughly `$1B` worldwide market growing 15-20% annually. ResMed's FY2005 revenue
was `$425.5M`, and Fisher & Paykel Healthcare reported `$65.7M` of OSA product revenue, anchoring a
large but still expanding global therapy revenue pool.

## Adoption Path

The market grew from a diagnosis bottleneck into a chronic home-therapy market. In 1995, ResMed cited
roughly 1,200 US sleep clinics, up from about 100 in 1985, but still inadequate for the undiagnosed
OSA population. By 2005, the core adoption loop was well underway: more sleep clinics and physician
awareness, more diagnosed patients, payer reimbursement for home equipment, better masks and quieter
devices, then recurring replacement of masks, cushions, tubing, filters, and humidification parts.

The key underestimated feature in hindsight is the recurrence. CPAP was not a one-time device sale.
It became a durable installed-base business because therapy is nightly and long term, accessories
wear out, and payers developed resupply schedules. Connectivity and cloud adherence later
strengthened reimbursement and provider workflow, but the economic engine was already visible before
2005 through lifetime therapy, mask replacement, and third-party reimbursement dependence.

## Market Structure

The 10-year HHI estimate of `0.23` implies an oligopoly with roughly four effective competitors.
Respironics and ResMed were the two most important global competitors, Fisher & Paykel Healthcare had
a smaller but fast-growing OSA product position, and several respiratory-device suppliers served
regional or product-specific niches.

The market did not become a single-winner device monopoly because PAP therapy is purchased through
reimbursed durable-medical-equipment channels, hospital and sleep-clinic influence, regional
reimbursement rules, and tendering. At the same time, it became more concentrated than ordinary
durable medical equipment because trust, clinical familiarity, patient comfort, scale manufacturing,
and regulatory quality systems all rewarded incumbents.

## Players

Philips Respironics was the largest 10-year participant. Respironics was already the larger OSA
competitor in the mid-1990s and reported record FY2005 net sales, with management describing
continued leadership in the global OSA marketplace. Philips later owned the franchise.

ResMed was the focused public CPAP innovator and the other clear global leader. Its FY2005 revenue
and flow-generator, mask, and accessory growth show it had scaled from a small 1995 base into a major
international OSA franchise.

Fisher & Paykel Healthcare captured a smaller but durable share through masks, humidification, and
flow generators. Its FY2005 OSA product revenue of `$65.7M` supports the `0.066` capture estimate.

Puritan Bennett/Tyco, DeVilbiss/Sunrise Medical, and other regional suppliers form the remainder.
They matter to HHI because they limit duopoly capture in tenders and local markets, but none matched
Respironics or ResMed globally by 2005.

its TAM before comparing to this benchmark.

The largest benchmark uncertainty is TAM boundary. PAP flow-generator market reports are narrower
than CPAP therapy economics because masks and accessories are economically important. Company segment
data can be broader than CPAP therapy because it includes adjacent respiratory products. The stored
`$1.0B` 10-year TAM is therefore deliberately rounded.
